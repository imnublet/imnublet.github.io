Covariance and correlation

Covariance measures how two variables move together linearly, and nothing more.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 25 min Y&G ch. 5
After this lesson you can
  • prove linearity of expectation for sums and the variance of a sum;
  • compute covariances using bilinearity, and correlation coefficients;
  • prove Cauchy–Schwarz and deduce $|\rho| \le 1$ with equality exactly for linear relations;
  • distinguish uncorrelated from independent, with counterexamples;
  • derive the best linear predictor of $Y$ from $X$ and its error $\sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)$.

§ 01 SumsLinearity of expectation, at last

Lesson 2.2 promised that $\E[X + Y] = \E[X] + \E[Y]$ needs the joint law to state. Now we have it.

Theorem 1linearity of expectation

If $\E|X|, \E|Y| < \infty$, then $\E[aX + bY] = a\,\E[X] + b\,\E[Y]$ for all constants $a, b$, whatever the dependence between $X$ and $Y$.

Proof

Jointly continuous case. By the two-dimensional LOTUS (lesson 3.1, Theorem 3(c)) with $g(x, y) = ax + by$, which is absolutely integrable because $|ax + by| \le |a||x| + |b||y|$,

$$\E[aX + bY] = a\iint x\, f_{X,Y} \d x\d y + b\iint y\, f_{X,Y} \d x\d y = a\int x f_X(x)\d x + b\int y f_Y(y)\d y,$$

where the inner integral over the other variable produces the marginal density (lesson 3.2, Theorem 1). The discrete case is the same with sums. ∎

No independence is needed, and that is what makes linearity so useful. Example: the number of fixed points of a uniformly random permutation of $n$ items is $\sum_{i=1}^n \ind_{A_i}$ with $A_i$ = "item $i$ stays put", and $\P[A_i] = (n-1)!/n! = 1/n$. The indicators are dependent, but $\E[\sum_i \ind_{A_i}] = n \cdot \tfrac1n = 1$ for every $n$.

§ 02 DefinitionCovariance

Definitioncovariance, correlation

If $\E[X^2], \E[Y^2] < \infty$, the covariance of $X$ and $Y$ is

$$\Cov[X, Y] = \E\big[(X - \mu_X)(Y - \mu_Y)\big] = \E[XY] - \mu_X\mu_Y. \tag{1}$$

If also $\sigma_X, \sigma_Y > 0$, the correlation coefficient is $\rho_{X,Y} = \Cov[X,Y]/(\sigma_X\sigma_Y)$. $X$ and $Y$ are uncorrelated if $\Cov[X,Y] = 0$.

The expectation $\E[XY]$ exists because $|xy| \le \tfrac12(x^2 + y^2)$. The second form in (1) comes from expanding the product and applying Theorem 1. A note on vocabulary: Yates & Goodman, like much of the signal-processing literature, call $\E[XY]$ itself the correlation of $X$ and $Y$, and say $X, Y$ are orthogonal if $\E[XY] = 0$. For zero-mean variables the two notions coincide.

Theorem 2rules for covariance
  1. $\Cov[X, X] = \Var[X]$ and $\Cov[X, Y] = \Cov[Y, X]$;
  2. bilinearity: $\Cov\big[\sum_i a_i X_i + c,\ \sum_j b_j Y_j + d\big] = \sum_i\sum_j a_i b_j \Cov[X_i, Y_j]$;
  3. $\displaystyle \Var\Big[\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\Big] = \sum_{i=1}^n \Var[X_i] + 2\sum_{i < j}\Cov[X_i, X_j]$;
  4. if $X$ and $Y$ are independent, $\Cov[X, Y] = 0$, so variances of independent summands add.
Proof

(a) is immediate from (1). (b) Constants drop out after centring: $\sum_i a_iX_i + c - \E[\cdot] = \sum_i a_i(X_i - \mu_i)$. So the covariance is $\E\big[\sum_i a_i(X_i - \mu_i)\sum_j b_j(Y_j - \nu_j)\big]$; expand the product and use Theorem 1 on the finite sum. (c) is (b) with $Y_j = X_j$, $a = b = \mathbf 1$: the double sum $\sum_{i,j}\Cov[X_i, X_j]$ has the $n$ diagonal terms $\Var[X_i]$ and each off-diagonal pair twice. (d) By lesson 3.2, Theorem 4, $\E[XY] = \E[X]\E[Y]$. ∎

Rule (c) with (d) gives $\Var[K] = np(1-p)$ for a binomial in one line, writing $K$ as a sum of $n$ independent Bernoulli indicators, and it is the whole proof of the $\sigma^2/n$ law for the sample mean in lesson 5.1.

§ 03 BoundCauchy–Schwarz and $|\rho| \le 1$

Theorem 3Cauchy–Schwarz inequality

If $\E[U^2], \E[V^2] < \infty$, then $\big(\E[UV]\big)^2 \le \E[U^2]\,\E[V^2]$, with equality if and only if $\P[U = tV] = 1$ for some constant $t$, or $\P[V = 0] = 1$.

Proof

If $\E[V^2] = 0$ then $V = 0$ almost surely (lesson 2.2, exercise 3), so $\E[UV] = 0$ and equality holds. Otherwise, for every real $t$, by Theorem 1,

$$0 \le \E\big[(U - tV)^2\big] = \E[U^2] - 2t\,\E[UV] + t^2\,\E[V^2].$$

Choose the minimising $t^* = \E[UV]/\E[V^2]$: the right side becomes $\E[U^2] - \E[UV]^2/\E[V^2] \ge 0$, which is the inequality. Equality means $\E[(U - t^*V)^2] = 0$, i.e. $U = t^*V$ almost surely. Conversely, if $U = tV$ almost surely, both sides equal $t^2\E[V^2]^2$. ∎

Corollaryrange of the correlation coefficient

$-1 \le \rho_{X,Y} \le 1$, and $|\rho_{X,Y}| = 1$ if and only if $Y = aX + b$ almost surely for constants $a \ne 0$, $b$, in which case $\rho = \operatorname{sign}(a)$.

Proof

Apply Theorem 3 to $U = Y - \mu_Y$, $V = X - \mu_X$: $\Cov[X,Y]^2 \le \sigma_X^2\sigma_Y^2$. Equality holds iff $Y - \mu_Y = t(X - \mu_X)$ almost surely; $t \ne 0$ since $\sigma_Y > 0$; then $\Cov = t\sigma_X^2$ and $\sigma_Y = |t|\sigma_X$, so $\rho = t/|t|$. ∎

So $\rho$ measures the strength of linear association on a fixed scale, and by bilinearity it is unchanged by $X \mapsto aX + b$ with $a > 0$ (units do not matter). It is the cosine of the angle between $X - \mu_X$ and $Y - \mu_Y$ in the inner product $\langle U, V \rangle = \E[UV]$, and Cauchy–Schwarz is exactly the statement that cosines lie in $[-1, 1]$.

§ 04 CautionUncorrelated is not independent

Independence implies zero covariance. The converse fails, and the failures are not exotic.

Examplea deterministic function with zero correlation

Let $X \sim \text{Uniform}(-1, 1)$ and $Y = X^2$. Then $\E[X] = 0$ and $\E[XY] = \E[X^3] = 0$ because $x^3$ is odd and the density is even, so $\Cov[X, Y] = 0$. Yet $Y$ is a function of $X$: knowing $X$ determines $Y$ exactly. The relation is perfectly quadratic, and covariance sees only the linear part, which here is zero.

The random-sign pair of lesson 3.2, $Y = SX$ with $X \sim \mathcal N(0, 1)$, is another: $\E[XY] = \E[S]\E[X^2] = 0$, but $|Y| = |X|$. For jointly Gaussian variables, and essentially only for them, uncorrelated does imply independent (lesson 3.6). Mutual information, distance correlation and the HSIC are dependence measures that vanish exactly under independence; covariance is not one of them.

§ 05 PredictionThe best linear predictor

Theorem 4linear least squares

Among all predictors of the form $\hat Y = aX + b$, the mean squared error $\E[(Y - aX - b)^2]$ is minimised by

$$a^* = \frac{\Cov[X, Y]}{\Var[X]} = \rho\frac{\sigma_Y}{\sigma_X}, \qquad b^* = \mu_Y - a^*\mu_X, \tag{2}$$

with minimum value $\sigma_Y^2(1 - \rho^2)$. The error $Y - \hat Y$ has mean zero and is uncorrelated with $X$.

Proof

For fixed $a$, the error $Y - aX - b$ is a random variable $W_a = Y - aX$ shifted by the constant $b$, so by the MSE identity of lesson 2.2, $\E[(W_a - b)^2] = \Var[W_a] + (\E[W_a] - b)^2$, minimised by $b = \E[W_a] = \mu_Y - a\mu_X$ with value $\Var[Y - aX]$. By Theorem 2,

$$\Var[Y - aX] = \sigma_Y^2 - 2a\Cov[X,Y] + a^2\sigma_X^2 = \sigma_X^2\Big(a - \frac{\Cov[X,Y]}{\sigma_X^2}\Big)^2 + \sigma_Y^2 - \frac{\Cov[X,Y]^2}{\sigma_X^2},$$

by completing the square. It is minimised at $a = a^*$ with value $\sigma_Y^2 - \rho^2\sigma_Y^2$. Finally $\E[Y - \hat Y] = 0$ by the choice of $b^*$, and $\Cov[X, Y - a^*X] = \Cov[X,Y] - a^*\sigma_X^2 = 0$. ∎

This is ordinary least-squares regression at the population level. The orthogonality of the error and $X$ is the normal equation; $\rho^2$ is the fraction of $\Var[Y]$ that the best line explains (the population $R^2$). Lesson 5.4 extends the result to vectors of observations, and lesson 3.5 shows that dropping the restriction to linear predictors gives the conditional mean.

§ 07 PracticeExercises

  1. Show $\Var[X - Y] = \Var[X] + \Var[Y] - 2\Cov[X,Y]$, and that $\Var[X + Y] = \Var[X - Y]$ iff $X, Y$ are uncorrelated.core

    Solution

    Theorem 2(b) with coefficients $(1, -1)$ gives the formula. Subtracting it from $\Var[X+Y] = \Var X + \Var Y + 2\Cov$ leaves $4\Cov[X,Y]$.

  2. For $X = \max(U_1, U_2)$ and $Y = \min(U_1, U_2)$ with $U_1, U_2$ independent Uniform$(0,1)$, show $\rho_{X,Y} = \tfrac12$.core

    Solution

    From the marginals $2x$ and $2(1-y)$: $\E[X] = \tfrac23$, $\E[Y] = \tfrac13$, $\E[X^2] = \tfrac12$, $\E[Y^2] = \tfrac16$, so $\Var[X] = \Var[Y] = \tfrac1{18}$. Since $XY = U_1U_2$, $\E[XY] = \tfrac14$ by independence. So $\Cov = \tfrac14 - \tfrac29 = \tfrac1{36}$ and $\rho = \frac{1/36}{1/18} = \tfrac12$.

  3. $X_1, \dots, X_n$ have common variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$. Show $\rho \ge -1/(n-1)$.core

    Solution

    $0 \le \Var[\sum_i X_i] = n\sigma^2 + n(n-1)\rho\sigma^2$, so $1 + (n-1)\rho \ge 0$. Many variables cannot all be strongly negatively correlated with one another.

  4. Let $Y = X + N$ where $X$ (signal) and $N$ (noise) are uncorrelated with variances $\sigma_X^2, \sigma_N^2$. Find $\rho_{X,Y}$ and the best linear estimate of $X$ from $Y$ when both have mean zero.core

    Solution

    $\Cov[X, Y] = \sigma_X^2$ and $\Var[Y] = \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_N^2$, so $\rho^2 = \frac{\sigma_X^2}{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_N^2} = \frac{\text{SNR}}{1 + \text{SNR}}$. By (2) with the roles of $X, Y$ swapped, $\hat X = \frac{\sigma_X^2}{\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_N^2} Y$: shrink the observation towards the prior mean by the SNR factor. This is the scalar Wiener filter.

  5. Show that if $X, Y$ take only two values each, then uncorrelated implies independent.stretch

    Solution

    By bilinearity covariance is unchanged under affine maps up to a non-zero factor, so we may assume $X, Y \in \{0, 1\}$. Then $\Cov = \P[X{=}1, Y{=}1] - \P[X{=}1]\P[Y{=}1] = 0$ says the events $\{X = 1\}$, $\{Y = 1\}$ are independent, and by lesson 1.4, Theorem 1 so are their complements in every combination. That is the factorisation of the joint PMF.
