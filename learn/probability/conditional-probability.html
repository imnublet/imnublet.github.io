Conditional probability and Bayes' rule

Learning that $B$ happened shrinks the world to $B$. Everything else is renormalisation.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 25 min Y&G ch. 1
After this lesson you can
  • define $\P[A \mid B]$ and prove it is itself a probability measure;
  • factor a joint probability with the chain rule;
  • compute a probability by splitting into cases with the law of total probability;
  • invert a conditional with Bayes' rule, in both probability and odds form, and update sequentially.

§ 01 DefinitionConditioning shrinks the sample space

Suppose a run of the experiment has happened and we are told only that the event $B$ occurred. Which probability should we now give $A$? Think in frequencies: over $N$ runs, $B$ occurs $N_B$ times and $A$ and $B$ together $N_{AB}$ times. Among the runs where $B$ occurred, the fraction where $A$ also occurred is

$$\frac{N_{AB}}{N_B} = \frac{N_{AB}/N}{N_B/N} \approx \frac{\P[A \cap B]}{\P[B]}.$$

The right-hand side is taken as the definition.

Definitionconditional probability

For events $A, B$ with $\P[B] > 0$, the conditional probability of $A$ given $B$ is

$$\P[A \mid B] = \frac{\P[A \cap B]}{\P[B]}. \tag{1}$$

Geometrically: discard every outcome outside $B$, keep the relative weights of the outcomes inside $B$, and rescale them so they sum to one. On a finite space of equally likely outcomes this is literally $\P[A \mid B] = |A \cap B| / |B|$: uniform on the smaller space $B$.

Theorem 1conditioning gives a probability measure

Fix $B$ with $\P[B] > 0$. Then $\P_B[\,\cdot\,] = \P[\,\cdot \mid B]$ satisfies the three axioms, and $\P_B[B] = 1$.

Proof

Non-negativity: a ratio of a non-negative and a positive number. Normalisation: $\P_B[\Omega] = \P[\Omega \cap B]/\P[B] = \P[B]/\P[B] = 1$, and the same computation gives $\P_B[B] = 1$. Countable additivity: if the $A_i$ are pairwise disjoint, so are the $A_i \cap B$, and by distributivity $\big(\bigcup_i A_i\big) \cap B = \bigcup_i (A_i \cap B)$. So

$$\P_B\Big[\bigcup_i A_i\Big] = \frac{\P\big[\bigcup_i (A_i \cap B)\big]}{\P[B]} = \frac{\sum_i \P[A_i \cap B]}{\P[B]} = \sum_i \P_B[A_i].$$

∎

Consequently every rule from lesson 1.2 holds verbatim with "$\mid B$" appended everywhere: $\P[A^c \mid B] = 1 - \P[A \mid B]$, conditional inclusion–exclusion, the conditional union bound, and so on. A useful corollary (exercise 5): conditioning on $B$ and then on $C$ is the same as conditioning on $B \cap C$.

Worked example 1.3.1conditioning on the sum of two dice
Problem

Two fair dice are rolled. Find the probability that at least one die shows a $6$, (a) unconditionally and (b) given that the sum is $8$.

Approach

Use the equally likely sample space of ordered pairs. Conditioning on $B$ with equally likely outcomes reduces to counting inside $B$: $\P[A \mid B] = \P[AB]/\P[B] = |AB|/|B|$.

Solution

  1. Sample space. $\Omega = \{1, \dots, 6\}^2$, $|\Omega| = 36$, all outcomes equally likely. Let $A$ = "at least one $6$" and $B$ = "sum is $8$".

  2. Unconditional. $A^c$ = "no $6$" $= \{1, \dots, 5\}^2$ has $25$ outcomes, so $\P[A] = 1 - \frac{25}{36} = \frac{11}{36} \approx 0.306$.

  3. List the conditioning event. $B = \{(2,6), (3,5), (4,4), (5,3), (6,2)\}$, so $\P[B] = \frac5{36}$.

  4. Intersect and divide. $A \cap B = \{(2, 6), (6, 2)\}$, so $$\P[A \mid B] = \frac{\P[A \cap B]}{\P[B]} = \frac{2/36}{5/36} = \frac25.$$

Result

$\P[A] = \frac{11}{36} \approx 0.31$ but $\P[A \mid B] = \frac25 = 0.4$. Learning that the sum is $8$ removes every outcome with a $1$, which makes a $6$ more likely. The $36$ disappears from the answer: conditioning made $B$ the new sample space, with its $5$ outcomes still equally likely.

§ 02 FactoringThe chain rule

Rearranging (1) gives $\P[A \cap B] = \P[A \mid B]\,\P[B]$. This is the more useful direction: experiments that unfold in stages specify conditional probabilities directly, and we multiply along the way.

Theorem 2chain rule

If $\P[A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{n-1}] > 0$, then

$$\P[A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_n] = \P[A_1]\, \prod_{k=2}^{n} \P\big[A_k \,\big|\, A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_{k-1}\big]. \tag{2}$$
Proof

Write $C_k = A_1 \cap \cdots \cap A_k$. Since $C_{n-1} \subseteq C_{k}$ for $k \le n-1$, monotonicity gives $\P[C_k] \ge \P[C_{n-1}] > 0$, so every conditional in (2) is defined. By (1), $\P[A_k \mid C_{k-1}] = \P[C_k]/\P[C_{k-1}]$, and the product telescopes:

$$\P[C_1] \prod_{k=2}^{n} \frac{\P[C_k]}{\P[C_{k-1}]} = \P[C_n].$$

∎

Examplethree aces

Deal three cards from a well-shuffled 52-card deck. With $A_k$ = "card $k$ is an ace", the chain rule gives $\P[A_1A_2A_3] = \frac{4}{52}\cdot\frac{3}{51}\cdot\frac{2}{50} = \frac{1}{5525}$. Each factor is read off the state of the deck after the previous draws; no sample space of $52 \cdot 51 \cdot 50$ ordered triples needs to be written down.

§ 03 CasesThe law of total probability

Combine the partition decomposition from lesson 1.1 with the chain rule and we can compute a probability by cases.

Theorem 3law of total probability

Let $\{B_1, B_2, \dots\}$ be a partition of $\Omega$ with $\P[B_i] > 0$ for every $i$. For any event $A$,

$$\P[A] = \sum_i \P[A \mid B_i]\,\P[B_i]. \tag{3}$$
Proof

By Theorem 2 of lesson 1.1, $A$ is the disjoint union of the $A \cap B_i$, so countable additivity gives $\P[A] = \sum_i \P[A \cap B_i]$, and each term equals $\P[A \mid B_i]\P[B_i]$ by (1). ∎

(If some $B_i$ has probability zero, simply drop it: $\P[A \cap B_i] \le \P[B_i] = 0$, so it adds nothing.) Formula (3) says $\P[A]$ is a weighted average of the conditional probabilities, with the probabilities of the cases as weights; in particular it lies between the smallest and largest $\P[A \mid B_i]$.

§ 04 InversionBayes' rule

Models are usually specified forwards, cause to effect: $\P[\text{symptom} \mid \text{disease}]$, $\P[\text{received bit} \mid \text{sent bit}]$. Inference runs backwards: we observe the effect and ask about the cause. Bayes' rule is the inversion.

Theorem 4Bayes' rule

Under the hypotheses of Theorem 3, and if $\P[A] > 0$,

$$\P[B_j \mid A] = \frac{\P[A \mid B_j]\,\P[B_j]}{\sum_i \P[A \mid B_i]\,\P[B_i]}. \tag{4}$$
Proof

By (1) twice, $\P[B_j \mid A]\,\P[A] = \P[A \cap B_j] = \P[A \mid B_j]\,\P[B_j]$. Divide by $\P[A]$ and expand it with (3). ∎

The vocabulary: $\P[B_j]$ is the prior, $\P[A \mid B_j]$ as a function of $j$ is the likelihood, $\P[B_j \mid A]$ is the posterior, and the denominator is the evidence, which only normalises. Hence the slogan posterior $\propto$ likelihood $\times$ prior.

With just two hypotheses $B$ and $B^c$, the normaliser cancels if we work with odds, $O(B) = \P[B]/\P[B^c]$.

Theorem 5Bayes' rule in odds form $$\underbrace{\frac{\P[B \mid A]}{\P[B^c \mid A]}}_{\text{posterior odds}} = \underbrace{\frac{\P[A \mid B]}{\P[A \mid B^c]}}_{\text{likelihood ratio } \Lambda(A)} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\P[B]}{\P[B^c]}}_{\text{prior odds}}. \tag{5}$$
Proof

Write (4) for $B_j = B$ and for $B_j = B^c$ (the partition $\{B, B^c\}$) and divide; the common denominator $\P[A]$ cancels. ∎

Taking logs, $\log O(B \mid A) = \log \Lambda(A) + \log O(B)$: evidence adds on the log-odds scale. If observations $A_1, \dots, A_n$ are conditionally independent given $B$ and given $B^c$ (meaning $\P[A_1 \cdots A_n \mid B] = \prod_k \P[A_k \mid B]$, and likewise for $B^c$; see lesson 1.4), the likelihood ratio of the joint observation factorises and

$$\log O(B \mid A_1 \cdots A_n) = \log O(B) + \sum_{k=1}^n \log \Lambda(A_k). \tag{6}$$

This is the computation inside a naive Bayes classifier and inside a sequential probability ratio test.

Examplea good test for a rare condition

A condition $D$ has prevalence $\P[D] = 0.01$. A test has sensitivity $\P[+ \mid D] = 0.95$ and specificity $\P[- \mid D^c] = 0.95$. By (4),

$$\P[D \mid +] = \frac{0.95 \cdot 0.01}{0.95 \cdot 0.01 + 0.05 \cdot 0.99} = \frac{0.0095}{0.0590} \approx 0.161.$$

In odds: prior odds $1/99$, likelihood ratio $0.95/0.05 = 19$, posterior odds $19/99$, i.e. probability $19/118$. A positive result raises the probability sixteen-fold, but most positives are still false, because healthy people outnumber sick ones 99 to 1 and 5% of a large number is large. Ignoring the prior here is the base-rate fallacy.

Worked example 1.3.2where do mislabelled examples come from?
Problem

A training set is labelled by three annotators: $A_1$ labels $50\%$ of the examples, $A_2$ labels $30\%$, $A_3$ labels $20\%$. Their error rates are $2\%$, $4\%$ and $10\%$. (a) What fraction of labels is wrong? (b) An audit finds a wrong label. What is the probability it came from $A_3$?

Approach

The annotators partition the examples, and the error rates are probabilities given the annotator. Part (a) is a total-probability calculation over that partition; part (b) reverses the conditioning, which is Bayes' rule, and its denominator is the answer to (a).

Solution

  1. Name events and translate the data. Let $B_i$ = "labelled by $A_i$" and $W$ = "label is wrong". Then $\{B_1, B_2, B_3\}$ is a partition with $\P[B_1] = 0.5$, $\P[B_2] = 0.3$, $\P[B_3] = 0.2$, and $\P[W \mid B_1] = 0.02$, $\P[W \mid B_2] = 0.04$, $\P[W \mid B_3] = 0.10$.

  2. Total probability. By Theorem 3, $$\P[W] = \sum_{i=1}^3 \P[W \mid B_i]\,\P[B_i] = 0.02 \cdot 0.5 + 0.04 \cdot 0.3 + 0.10 \cdot 0.2 = 0.010 + 0.012 + 0.020 = 0.042.$$

  3. Bayes. By Theorem 4, $$\P[B_3 \mid W] = \frac{\P[W \mid B_3]\,\P[B_3]}{\P[W]} = \frac{0.020}{0.042} = \frac{10}{21} \approx 0.476.$$

  4. Sanity check. The same computation gives $\P[B_1 \mid W] = \frac{10}{42} = \frac{5}{21}$ and $\P[B_2 \mid W] = \frac{12}{42} = \frac{6}{21}$. The three posteriors sum to $1$, as they must, since $\P[\,\cdot \mid W]$ is a probability measure (Theorem 1).

Result

(a) $4.2\%$ of labels are wrong. (b) $\P[B_3 \mid W] = 10/21 \approx 0.48$: the annotator who labels only a fifth of the data is behind almost half of the errors. The posterior weights are just the three terms of the total-probability sum, normalised; computing that table once answers both parts.

§ 06 PracticeExercises

  1. A binary channel sends $0$ with probability $0.6$ and $1$ with probability $0.4$. A sent $0$ is received as $1$ with probability $0.1$; a sent $1$ is received as $0$ with probability $0.2$. Find $\P[\text{receive } 1]$ and $\P[\text{sent } 1 \mid \text{receive } 1]$.core

    Solution

    By (3), $\P[R_1] = 0.1 \cdot 0.6 + 0.8 \cdot 0.4 = 0.06 + 0.32 = 0.38$. By (4), $\P[S_1 \mid R_1] = 0.32 / 0.38 \approx 0.842$.

  2. Let $\P[A], \P[B] > 0$. Show that $\P[A \mid B] > \P[A]$ if and only if $\P[B \mid A] > \P[B]$. (Positive association is symmetric.)core

    Solution

    Both inequalities are equivalent to $\P[A \cap B] > \P[A]\,\P[B]$: multiply the first by $\P[B] > 0$, the second by $\P[A] > 0$, and use (1).

  3. A prize is behind one of three doors, uniformly at random. You pick door 1. The host, who knows where the prize is, opens one of doors 2 and 3 that does not hide it, choosing uniformly if both are empty. Given that the host opens door 3, what is the probability the prize is behind door 2?core

    Solution

    Let $C$ be the prize door and $H$ the opened door. The likelihoods are $\P[H{=}3 \mid C{=}1] = 1/2$, $\P[H{=}3 \mid C{=}2] = 1$ (door 3 is the only empty one he may open), $\P[H{=}3 \mid C{=}3] = 0$. By (3), $\P[H{=}3] = \tfrac13(\tfrac12 + 1 + 0) = \tfrac12$, and by (4) $\P[C{=}2 \mid H{=}3] = \tfrac{1 \cdot 1/3}{1/2} = \tfrac23$. Switching wins with probability $2/3$. Note how the host's rule enters through the likelihood: a host who opens a door at random (and happens to reveal no prize) gives $1/2$ instead.

  4. In the diagnostic example, a patient tests positive twice, with the two results conditionally independent given $D$ and given $D^c$. Find $\P[D \mid {+}{+}]$.core

    Solution

    By (6) the posterior odds are $\frac{1}{99} \cdot 19^2 = \frac{361}{99}$, so $\P[D \mid {+}{+}] = \frac{361}{361 + 99} = \frac{361}{460} \approx 0.785$.

  5. Let $\P[B \cap C] > 0$. Show that $\P_B[A \mid C] = \P[A \mid B \cap C]$, where $\P_B$ is the measure of Theorem 1. Conclude that Bayes' rule may be applied inside a conditional world.stretch

    Solution

    $\P_B[C] = \P[B \cap C]/\P[B] > 0$, so the left side is defined, and by (1) applied to $\P_B$, $$\P_B[A \mid C] = \frac{\P_B[A \cap C]}{\P_B[C]} = \frac{\P[A \cap C \cap B]/\P[B]}{\P[C \cap B]/\P[B]} = \P[A \mid B \cap C].$$ Since $\P_B$ is a probability measure, every theorem of this lesson holds for it; rewritten with this identity, it holds with an extra "$\cap B$" in every conditioning event.
