Marginals and independence

Integrate out what you do not care about. If the joint law factorises, nothing was lost.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 25 min Y&G ch. 5
After this lesson you can
  • obtain marginal PMFs and densities from a joint law, and prove the formulas;
  • construct different joint laws with the same marginals;
  • define independence of random variables and test it by factorising the PMF, CDF or density;
  • use $\E[g(X)h(Y)] = \E[g(X)]\,\E[h(Y)]$ for independent $X, Y$.

§ 01 MarginalsIntegrating out a variable

The joint law contains the law of each component. Recovering it is called marginalising, after the old practice of writing row and column totals in the margins of a joint table.

Theorem 1marginal PMF and density

If $X, Y$ are discrete,

$$P_X(x) = \sum_{y \in S_Y} P_{X,Y}(x, y), \qquad P_Y(y) = \sum_{x \in S_X} P_{X,Y}(x, y). \tag{1}$$

If $X, Y$ are jointly continuous, $X$ and $Y$ are continuous with

$$f_X(x) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_{X,Y}(x, y) \d y, \qquad f_Y(y) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_{X,Y}(x, y) \d x. \tag{2}$$
Proof

Discrete. The events $\{Y = y\}$, $y \in S_Y$, partition $\Omega$, so $\{X = x\}$ is the disjoint union of $\{X = x, Y = y\}$ over $y$, and countable additivity gives (1).

Continuous. By Theorem 1(c) of lesson 3.1 and (4) there, then exchanging the order of integration (Tonelli, the integrand is non-negative),

$$F_X(x) = \lim_{y \to \infty} F_{X,Y}(x, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{x} \Big(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_{X,Y}(u, v) \d v\Big) \d u,$$

where the limit passes inside by monotone convergence. This exhibits $F_X$ as the integral of the inner function, which is therefore a density of $X$. The formula for $f_Y$ is symmetric. ∎

For the triangle density $f_{X,Y} = 2$ on $\{0 \le y \le x \le 1\}$ of lesson 3.1: for fixed $x \in [0,1]$ the density is $2$ for $y \in [0, x]$, so $f_X(x) = 2x$; for fixed $y$ it is $2$ for $x \in [y, 1]$, so $f_Y(y) = 2(1 - y)$. The larger of two uniforms leans right and the smaller leans left, as they should.

Marginals lose information

The map from joint laws to pairs of marginals is many-to-one. Let $X$ be Bernoulli$(\tfrac12)$. Taking $Y$ an independent copy, or $Y = X$, or $Y = 1 - X$ gives three joint PMFs, putting mass $\tfrac14$ on each of the four cells, or $\tfrac12$ on the diagonal, or $\tfrac12$ on the anti-diagonal. All three have Bernoulli$(\tfrac12)$ marginals. The part of the joint law that the marginals do not determine is the dependence structure; in continuous models it is called the copula.

§ 02 DefinitionIndependent random variables

Lesson 1.4 defined independence for events. Random variables are independent when every event about one is independent of every event about the other.

Definitionindependence

$X$ and $Y$ are independent if for all Borel sets $A, B \subseteq \R$,

$$\P[X \in A,\; Y \in B] = \P[X \in A]\;\P[Y \in B]. \tag{3}$$

Checking (3) for every pair of sets is impractical. The following criteria reduce it to one function.

Theorem 2factorisation criteria

$X$ and $Y$ are independent if and only if any one of the following holds:

  1. $F_{X,Y}(x, y) = F_X(x)\,F_Y(y)$ for all $x, y$;
  2. (discrete case) $P_{X,Y}(x, y) = P_X(x)\,P_Y(y)$ for all $x, y$;
  3. (jointly continuous case) $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_X(x)\,f_Y(y)$ for all $(x, y)$ outside a set of zero area.
Proof

(3) implies (a) with $A = (-\infty, x]$, $B = (-\infty, y]$, and (b) with $A = \{x\}$, $B = \{y\}$.

(b) implies (3): by (3) of lesson 3.1 applied to $A \times B$, $\P[X \in A, Y \in B] = \sum_{x \in A}\sum_{y \in B} P_X(x)P_Y(y) = \big(\sum_{x \in A}P_X(x)\big)\big(\sum_{y \in B}P_Y(y)\big)$.

(c) implies (3): by Theorem 3(b) of lesson 3.1 and Tonelli, $\P[X \in A, Y \in B] = \int_A\int_B f_X(x)f_Y(y)\d y\d x = \int_A f_X \cdot \int_B f_Y$.

(a) implies (c): differentiate $F_X(x)F_Y(y)$ in $x$ and $y$; at points where everything is continuous this gives $f_X(x)f_Y(y) = \partial^2 F_{X,Y}/\partial x \partial y = f_{X,Y}(x,y)$.

(a) implies (3) in general: the two sides of (3), viewed as functions of $A \times B$, both extend to probability measures on $\R^2$ (the law of $(X,Y)$ and the product of the marginal laws), and by (a) and the rectangle formula they agree on all rectangles. Measures that agree on rectangles agree on all Borel sets (Dynkin's $\pi$–$\lambda$ theorem). This step we take from measure theory. ∎

Theorem 3product form on a product support

If $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = g(x)\,h(y)$ for all $(x, y)$, with $g, h \ge 0$, then $X$ and $Y$ are independent, and $f_X = g / \int g$, $f_Y = h / \int h$.

Proof

Let $G = \int g$ and $H = \int h$. Then $1 = \iint g(x)h(y) = GH$, so both are finite and positive. By (2), $f_X(x) = g(x)H = g(x)/G$ and $f_Y(y) = h(y)G = h(y)/H$, so $f_X(x)f_Y(y) = g(x)h(y)/(GH) = f_{X,Y}(x,y)$; apply Theorem 2(c). ∎

The hypothesis is "for all $(x, y)$", including where the density is zero. The triangle density looks like a product, $2 = 2 \cdot 1$, but only on the triangle; on all of $\R^2$ it is $2\,\ind\{0 \le y \le x \le 1\}$, which does not split. Indeed $f_X(x)f_Y(y) = 4x(1 - y) \ne f_{X,Y}(x,y)$, and $X, Y$ are dependent: knowing $Y = 0.9$ forces $X \ge 0.9$. A support that is not a product set always means dependence.

Worked example 3.2.1marginals from a table, and a factorisation check
Problem

$X \in \{0, 1\}$ and $Y \in \{0, 1, 2\}$ have joint PMF

$y = 0$$y = 1$$y = 2$
$x = 0$$0.10$$0.20$$0.10$
$x = 1$$0.15$$0.30$$0.15$

Find the marginal PMFs and decide whether $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

Approach

Marginals are row and column sums (1). Independence in the discrete case is Theorem 2(b): every cell must equal the product of its row and column marginals. One failing cell disproves it; all six must be checked to prove it.

Solution

  1. Row sums. $P_X(0) = 0.10 + 0.20 + 0.10 = 0.4$, $P_X(1) = 0.15 + 0.30 + 0.15 = 0.6$.

  2. Column sums. $P_Y(0) = 0.25$, $P_Y(1) = 0.50$, $P_Y(2) = 0.25$.

  3. Check every cell. Row $x = 0$: $0.4 \times (0.25, 0.5, 0.25) = (0.10, 0.20, 0.10)$ ✓. Row $x = 1$: $0.6 \times (0.25, 0.5, 0.25) = (0.15, 0.30, 0.15)$ ✓.

Result

$P_X = (0.4, 0.6)$, $P_Y = (0.25, 0.5, 0.25)$, and $X, Y$ are independent. A quick visual test: a table is a product of marginals exactly when all its rows are proportional to one another (here row 2 = $1.5 \times$ row 1).

Worked example 3.2.2the density factors, yet the variables are dependent
Problem

$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = 8xy$ on $T = \{0 \le y \le x \le 1\}$ and $0$ elsewhere (worked example 3.1.2). Find $f_X$ and $f_Y$, and decide whether $X$ and $Y$ are independent.

Approach

Integrate out each variable with (2), keeping the support in the limits. For independence, the formula $8xy$ looks like a product $g(x)h(y)$, but Theorem 3 requires the product form for all $(x, y)$, indicator of the support included.

Solution

  1. Marginal of $X$. For $0 \le x \le 1$, $y$ runs over $[0, x]$: $f_X(x) = \int_0^x 8xy \d y = 4x^3$.

  2. Marginal of $Y$. For $0 \le y \le 1$, $x$ runs over $[y, 1]$: $f_Y(y) = \int_y^1 8xy \d x = 4y(1 - y^2)$. Check: $\int_0^1 4y - 4y^3 \d y = 2 - 1 = 1$ ✓.

  3. Test the factorisation. Take $(x, y) = (0.2, 0.5)$, outside $T$: $f_{X,Y} = 0$ but $f_X(0.2)\,f_Y(0.5) = 0.032 \times 1.5 = 0.048 > 0$. The inequality holds on the whole open triangle $\{0 < x < y < 1\}$, which has area $\frac12$, so Theorem 2(c) fails.

  4. Why Theorem 3 does not apply. $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = 8xy\,\ind\{y \le x\}$ on the unit square, and $\ind\{y \le x\}$ does not split into a function of $x$ times a function of $y$.

Result

$f_X(x) = 4x^3$, $f_Y(y) = 4y(1 - y^2)$ on $[0, 1]$, and $X, Y$ are dependent. Knowing $X = x$ confines $Y$ to $[0, x]$. The support of an independent pair must be a product set (a rectangle, possibly infinite); a triangular support rules independence out before any algebra.

§ 03 ConsequencesWhat independence buys

Theorem 4functions and products of independent variables

Let $X, Y$ be independent. Then (a) $g(X)$ and $h(Y)$ are independent for any (Borel) functions $g, h$; and (b) if $\E|g(X)|$ and $\E|h(Y)|$ are finite,

$$\E[g(X)\,h(Y)] = \E[g(X)]\;\E[h(Y)]. \tag{4}$$
Proof

(a) $\{g(X) \in A\} = \{X \in g^{-1}(A)\}$ and similarly for $h$, so (3) for $X, Y$ with the sets $g^{-1}(A)$, $h^{-1}(B)$ is (3) for $g(X), h(Y)$.

(b) In the continuous case, by Theorem 3(c) of lesson 3.1 and the factorisation, $\E|g(X)h(Y)| = \iint |g(x)||h(y)| f_X(x) f_Y(y) = \E|g(X)|\,\E|h(Y)| < \infty$ by Tonelli, so Fubini allows the same computation without absolute values, giving $\int g f_X \cdot \int h f_Y$. The discrete case is the same with sums. ∎

Everything extends to $n$ variables: $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are mutually independent if $\P[X_1 \in A_1, \dots, X_n \in A_n] = \prod_i \P[X_i \in A_i]$ for all sets $A_i$, equivalently if the joint PMF, CDF or density factorises into $n$ marginals. When in addition all $X_i$ have the same law they are independent and identically distributed, written $X_1, \dots, X_n \iid X$: the standard model of a sample in statistics and of a training set in machine learning.

Worked example 3.2.3which arrives first, an exponential or a uniform?
Problem

$X \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and $Y \sim \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ are independent. Find $\P[X < Y]$ and $\E[XY^2]$.

Approach

Independence gives the joint density as a product, so $\P[X < Y]$ is a double integral over a region. Integrate first over the variable whose limits depend on the other. For $\E[XY^2]$, Theorem 4 avoids any double integral.

Solution

  1. Joint density. By Theorem 2(c), $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = e^{-x}$ for $x \ge 0$, $0 < y < 1$, and $0$ otherwise.

  2. Set up. $\{X < Y\}$: for each $y \in (0, 1)$, $x \in [0, y)$. So $\P[X < Y] = \int_0^1 \int_0^y e^{-x} \d x \d y = \int_0^1 (1 - e^{-y})\d y$.

  3. Integrate. $\int_0^1 (1 - e^{-y})\d y = 1 - (1 - e^{-1}) = e^{-1} \approx 0.368$. Equivalently, $\P[X < Y] = \E[F_X(Y)]$: condition on $Y$ and average.

  4. Product moment. By (4), $\E[XY^2] = \E[X]\,\E[Y^2] = 1 \cdot \frac13 = \frac13$.

Result

$\P[X < Y] = e^{-1}$ and $\E[XY^2] = \frac13$. Note the order of integration: putting $y$ outside made the inner limits $[0, y]$ simple; the other order would need the split $x < 1$ and $x \ge 1$.

§ 04 In codeTesting a factorisation

§ 05 PracticeExercises

  1. Let $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$ and $Y \sim \text{Exp}(\mu)$ be independent. Show $\P[X < Y] = \lambda/(\lambda + \mu)$.core

    Solution

    By Theorem 2(c), $f_{X,Y} = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\mu e^{-\mu y}$ on $x, y \ge 0$. Integrate over $\{x < y\}$ with $x$ outermost: $\int_0^\infty \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\big(\int_x^\infty \mu e^{-\mu y}\d y\big)\d x = \int_0^\infty \lambda e^{-(\lambda + \mu)x}\d x = \frac{\lambda}{\lambda + \mu}$. This is the "race" between two exponential clocks used in queueing and in the Gillespie algorithm.

  2. With $X, Y$ as in exercise 1, show $\min(X, Y) \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda + \mu)$.core

    Solution

    $\P[\min(X,Y) > t] = \P[X > t, Y > t] = e^{-\lambda t}e^{-\mu t}$ by (3) with $A = B = (t, \infty)$.

  3. The joint density is $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = 6e^{-2x - 3y}$ for $x, y \ge 0$ and $0$ otherwise. Are $X, Y$ independent? Find $\E[XY]$.core

    Solution

    On all of $\R^2$ the density is $\big(2e^{-2x}\ind\{x \ge 0\}\big)\big(3e^{-3y}\ind\{y \ge 0\}\big)$, a product over the product support $[0,\infty)^2$. By Theorem 3, $X \sim \text{Exp}(2)$ and $Y \sim \text{Exp}(3)$ are independent, and by (4) $\E[XY] = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac13 = \tfrac16$.

  4. Let $X, Y$ be independent, each $\pm 1$ with probability $\tfrac12$, and $Z = XY$. Show $X, Y, Z$ are pairwise independent but not mutually independent.core

    Solution

    $Z$ is also $\pm1$ with probability $\tfrac12$. For each of the four values of $(x, z)$, $\{X = x, Z = z\} = \{X = x, Y = xz\}$ has probability $\tfrac14 = P_X(x)P_Z(z)$; likewise for $(Y, Z)$. But $\P[X = 1, Y = 1, Z = -1] = 0 \ne \tfrac18$. This is the coin example of lesson 1.4 in $\pm1$ notation, and the reason XOR-like features defeat models that only look at pairs.

  5. Show that if $X$ and $Y$ are independent and $Y = X$ almost surely, then $X$ is almost surely constant.stretch

    Solution

    For every $x$, $F_X(x) = \P[X \le x, Y \le x] = F_X(x)F_Y(x) = F_X(x)^2$, so $F_X(x) \in \{0, 1\}$. A CDF taking only the values $0$ and $1$ jumps from $0$ to $1$ at a single point $c = \inf\{x : F_X(x) = 1\}$, and then $\P[X = c] = 1$.
