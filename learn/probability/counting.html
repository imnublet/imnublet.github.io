Counting methods

On a finite space of equally likely outcomes, probability is counting. Four sampling schemes cover almost every case.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 25 min Y&G ch. 2
After this lesson you can
  • prove the multiplication rule and derive from it the four sampling formulas;
  • count arrangements with multinomial coefficients and solutions of $x_1 + \dots + x_r = k$ with stars and bars;
  • prove binomial identities by double counting;
  • solve the birthday problem exactly and bound it in closed form;
  • derive the hypergeometric law for sampling without replacement.

§ 01 PrincipleThe multiplication rule

When outcomes are equally likely, $\P[A] = |A|/|\Omega|$ (lesson 1.2, eq. 4), and probability reduces to counting two sets. Almost every count rests on one principle.

Theorem 1multiplication rule

Suppose each element of a set $S$ is built by a sequence of $k$ choices, where the first choice has $n_1$ options and, whatever the earlier choices were, choice $j$ has $n_j$ options, and distinct sequences of choices build distinct elements. Then $|S| = n_1 n_2 \cdots n_k$.

Proof

Induction on $k$. For $k = 1$ there are $n_1$ elements. For $k > 1$, group the elements of $S$ by their first choice. Each group is built by $k-1$ further choices with $n_2, \dots, n_k$ options, so by hypothesis has $n_2 \cdots n_k$ elements. The $n_1$ groups are disjoint (distinct sequences give distinct elements), so $|S| = n_1 \cdot n_2 \cdots n_k$. ∎

Note the theorem needs the number of options at step $j$ to be fixed, not the options themselves. That is exactly what happens when drawing without replacement.

§ 02 SamplingFour ways to draw $k$ from $n$

Draw $k$ items from $n$ distinguishable items. Two binary questions fix the count: is the order of the draws recorded, and is an item put back before the next draw?

Theorem 2ordered samples

(a) With replacement, the number of ordered $k$-samples is $n^k$.
(b) Without replacement ($k \le n$), it is the falling factorial

$$(n)_k = n(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1) = \frac{n!}{(n-k)!}, \tag{1}$$

and in particular there are $n!$ orderings (permutations) of $n$ items.

Proof

Apply Theorem 1 with $n_j = n$ in (a), and with $n_j = n - (j-1)$ in (b), since $j-1$ distinct items have already been removed when draw $j$ is made. ∎

Theorem 3unordered samples without replacement

The number of $k$-element subsets of an $n$-set is

$$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{(n)_k}{k!} = \frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}. \tag{2}$$
Proof

Count ordered samples without replacement in two ways. Directly, there are $(n)_k$. Alternatively, build one by first choosing the subset of items that appears ($\binom nk$ options, the unknown) and then an order of those $k$ items ($k!$ options, Theorem 2(b) with $n = k$). Each ordered sample arises from exactly one (subset, order) pair, so Theorem 1 gives $(n)_k = \binom nk\, k!$. ∎

This "count the same set two ways" argument, double counting, is the main proof technique of combinatorics. Two quick consequences: $\binom nk = \binom n{n-k}$ (choosing what to take is choosing what to leave), and Pascal's rule $\binom nk = \binom{n-1}{k-1} + \binom{n-1}{k}$ (split the $k$-subsets by whether they contain a fixed item).

Theorem 4binomial theorem $$(x + y)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom nk x^k y^{n-k}. \tag{3}$$
Proof

Expanding $(x+y)(x+y)\cdots(x+y)$ without collecting terms gives $2^n$ products, one per way of picking $x$ or $y$ from each of the $n$ factors. The product equals $x^k y^{n-k}$ exactly when $x$ was picked from a $k$-subset of the factors, and there are $\binom nk$ such subsets. ∎

The fourth scheme, unordered with replacement, counts multisets: how many times each item was drawn, regardless of order.

Theorem 5stars and bars

The number of multisets of size $k$ from $n$ types, equivalently of solutions of $x_1 + \cdots + x_n = k$ in non-negative integers, is

$$\binom{n + k - 1}{k}. \tag{4}$$
Proof

Encode a solution $(x_1, \dots, x_n)$ as a string of $k$ stars and $n-1$ bars: $x_1$ stars, a bar, $x_2$ stars, a bar, …, $x_n$ stars. For example $(2,0,1)$ with $k = 3$ becomes ★★||★. The map is a bijection between solutions and strings of length $n + k - 1$ with exactly $k$ stars: given such a string, the bars cut it into $n$ runs whose lengths are the $x_i$. Such a string is determined by the positions of its stars, a $k$-subset of $n+k-1$ positions, so Theorem 3 gives (4). ∎

Careful

Ordered samples with replacement are equally likely when the draws are independent and uniform; the multisets they collapse to are not. With $n = 2$, $k = 2$ the multiset $\{1,2\}$ arises from two ordered samples and $\{1,1\}$ from one. Use (4) to count, never to compute probabilities by $|A|/|\Omega|$ unless you have a reason to think multisets are equally likely (in physics, Bose–Einstein statistics is exactly that reason).

Worked example 1.5.1two people next to each other
Problem

Eight people, among them Alice and Bob, sit down in a random order on a row of $8$ chairs, all orders being equally likely. What is the probability that Alice and Bob sit next to each other?

Approach

The sample space is the set of seatings, i.e. ordered arrangements of $8$ people, so count with Theorem 2. For the event, the standard trick is to glue Alice and Bob into one block, count arrangements of blocks, then multiply by the arrangements inside the block. Confirm with a second, conditional, count.

Solution

  1. Sample space. A seating is a permutation of the $8$ people: $|\Omega| = 8! = 40\,320$ equally likely outcomes.

  2. Count the event. Treat "Alice and Bob" as one block. There are $7$ units to arrange, in $7!$ ways, and the block can be ordered as AB or BA. By the multiplication rule the event has $2 \cdot 7!$ outcomes.

  3. Divide.

    $$\P[\text{adjacent}] = \frac{2 \cdot 7!}{8!} = \frac{2}{8} = \frac14.$$

  4. Second method. Count the adjacent pairs of chairs instead: there are $7$ of them, $\{1,2\}, \dots, \{7, 8\}$. The pair of chairs Alice and Bob occupy is a uniformly random unordered pair out of $\binom82 = 28$ by symmetry, so the probability is $7/28 = 1/4$. ✓

Result

$\P[\text{adjacent}] = \frac14$. When a problem says "random order", the sample space is the $n!$ permutations; the block trick handles "together", and counting a coarser symmetric object (here, the pair of chairs) is often a quicker check.

§ 03 GroupsMultinomial coefficients

Theorem 6multinomial coefficient

The number of ways to split $n$ distinguishable items into $r$ labelled groups of sizes $n_1, \dots, n_r$, with $n_1 + \cdots + n_r = n$, is

$$\binom{n}{n_1, \dots, n_r} = \frac{n!}{n_1!\, n_2! \cdots n_r!}. \tag{5}$$
Proof

Fill the groups in turn: choose group 1 from all $n$ items, group 2 from the remaining $n - n_1$, and so on. By Theorems 1 and 3 the count is

$$\binom{n}{n_1}\binom{n - n_1}{n_2} \cdots \binom{n - n_1 - \cdots - n_{r-1}}{n_r} = \frac{n!}{n_1!\,(n-n_1)!} \cdot \frac{(n-n_1)!}{n_2!\,(n-n_1-n_2)!} \cdots \frac{n_r!}{n_r!\,0!},$$

and the product telescopes to (5). ∎

Equivalently, (5) counts the distinct words formed by rearranging $n$ letters among which letter $i$ repeats $n_i$ times. With $r = 2$ it is $\binom n{n_1}$, and the same expansion argument as in Theorem 4 proves the multinomial theorem $(x_1 + \cdots + x_r)^n = \sum \binom{n}{n_1, \dots, n_r} x_1^{n_1} \cdots x_r^{n_r}$.

§ 04 ApplicationThe birthday problem

Each of $k$ people has a birthday uniform on $n$ days, independently (ignore leap years and seasonal variation). Ordered birthday lists are then equally likely: $|\Omega| = n^k$. The lists with no repeat are ordered samples without replacement, so by Theorem 2

$$\P[\text{all distinct}] = \frac{(n)_k}{n^k} = \prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\Big(1 - \frac{i}{n}\Big). \tag{6}$$

For $n = 365$ this drops below $\tfrac12$ at $k = 23$ (it is $0.4927$), so with $23$ people a shared birthday is more likely than not. The intuition: what matters is the number of pairs, $\binom{23}{2} = 253$, not the number of people.

Theorem 7exponential bound $$\P[\text{all distinct}] \le \exp\Big(-\frac{k(k-1)}{2n}\Big). \tag{7}$$
Proof

The function $g(x) = e^{-x} - (1 - x)$ has $g(0) = 0$ and $g'(x) = 1 - e^{-x} \ge 0$ for $x \ge 0$, so $1 - x \le e^{-x}$ on $[0, \infty)$. Every factor in (6) is non-negative, so we may bound them one by one:

$$\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\Big(1 - \frac in\Big) \le \prod_{i=0}^{k-1} e^{-i/n} = \exp\Big(-\frac1n \sum_{i=0}^{k-1} i\Big) = \exp\Big(-\frac{k(k-1)}{2n}\Big).$$

∎

The bound is also a good approximation. Since $\log(1 - x) = -x - x^2/2 - \cdots$, the logarithm of (6) falls below the exponent in (7) by about $\sum_{i < k} i^2/2n^2 \approx k^3/6n^2$, which is small when $k \ll n^{2/3}$ (for $n = 365$, $k = 23$ the gap is $0.015$, and the two sides of (7) are $0.493$ and $0.500$). It tells us a collision becomes likely once $k \approx \sqrt{2n \ln 2} \approx 1.18\sqrt n$. The same $\sqrt n$ scaling governs hash collisions (birthday attacks need about $2^{b/2}$ tries against a $b$-bit hash) and duplicate draws in bootstrap resampling.

§ 05 ApplicationSampling without replacement: the hypergeometric law

An urn holds $N$ items, $K$ of them marked. Draw $m$ without replacement, all $\binom Nm$ subsets equally likely. The number of subsets with exactly $j$ marked items is, by Theorem 1, (ways to choose the $j$ marked) $\times$ (ways to choose the $m - j$ unmarked):

$$\P[J = j] = \frac{\binom Kj \binom{N-K}{m-j}}{\binom Nm}, \qquad \max(0, m - N + K) \le j \le \min(m, K). \tag{8}$$

This is the law behind quality-control sampling, Fisher's exact test and card-hand probabilities. When $N$ is large compared with $m$, removing a few items barely changes the urn, and (8) is close to the binomial law with $p = K/N$ (exercise 5).

Worked example 1.5.2matching three numbers in a lottery
Problem

A lottery draws $6$ numbers from $\{1, \dots, 49\}$ without replacement. You hold a ticket with $6$ distinct numbers. What is the probability that exactly $3$ of your numbers are drawn?

Approach

Order does not matter for the result, so the sample space is the set of unordered $6$-subsets, all equally likely. Split the $49$ numbers into "yours" ($6$) and "not yours" ($43$) and count draws by how many they take from each group: this is the hypergeometric law.

Solution

  1. Sample space. $|\Omega| = \binom{49}{6} = 13\,983\,816$ by Theorem 3.

  2. Count the event. A draw with exactly $3$ of your numbers chooses $3$ of your $6$ and $3$ of the other $43$. By the multiplication rule there are $$\binom{6}{3}\binom{43}{3} = 20 \cdot 12\,341 = 246\,820$$ such draws.

  3. Divide. $\P[\text{exactly } 3] = 246\,820 / 13\,983\,816 \approx 0.01765$, about $1$ in $57$.

Result

$\P = \binom63\binom{43}3 / \binom{49}6 \approx 0.0177$. Whenever a population splits into two types and we sample without replacement, the answer has this "choose from each group, divide by choose from all" shape.

§ 07 PracticeExercises

  1. Show that a 5-card poker hand is a full house (three of one rank, two of another) with probability $3744/\binom{52}{5} \approx 0.00144$.core

    Solution

    All $\binom{52}5 = 2\,598\,960$ hands are equally likely. Build a full house by Theorem 1: the rank of the triple (13), its 3 suits ($\binom43 = 4$), the rank of the pair (12 remaining), its 2 suits ($\binom42 = 6$). Distinct choices give distinct hands, so there are $13 \cdot 4 \cdot 12 \cdot 6 = 3744$. The order of the two ranks matters here (triple vs pair), which is why it is $13 \cdot 12$ and not $\binom{13}{2}$.

  2. Prove Vandermonde's identity $\sum_j \binom mj \binom n{k-j} = \binom{m+n}k$ by double counting, and conclude that the hypergeometric probabilities (8) sum to one.core

    Solution

    Count $k$-subsets of a set of $m$ red and $n$ blue items. Directly: $\binom{m+n}k$. Split by the number $j$ of red items: for each $j$ there are $\binom mj\binom n{k-j}$ subsets, and the classes are disjoint. Applied to $K$ marked and $N - K$ unmarked items and subsets of size $m$, the identity says that the numerators of (8) sum over $j$ to $\binom Nm$.

  3. How many solutions does $x_1 + \cdots + x_r = n$ have in positive integers?core

    Solution

    Substitute $y_i = x_i - 1 \ge 0$: $y_1 + \cdots + y_r = n - r$. By (4) with $r$ types and size $n - r$, the count is $\binom{n-1}{n-r} = \binom{n-1}{r-1}$. (Directly: choose $r-1$ of the $n-1$ gaps between $n$ stars to place bars.)

  4. Using only (7), find the smallest $k$ that guarantees $\P[\text{shared}] \ge \tfrac12$ for $n = 365$.core

    Solution

    We need $e^{-k(k-1)/730} \le \tfrac12$, i.e. $k(k-1) \ge 730 \ln 2 \approx 505.997$. Since $22 \cdot 21 = 462$ and $23 \cdot 22 = 506$, the answer is $k = 23$, which happens to be the exact answer too.

  5. Show that for fixed $m$, $j$ and $K/N \to p \in (0,1)$ as $N \to \infty$, the hypergeometric probability (8) converges to $\binom mj p^j (1-p)^{m-j}$.stretch

    Solution

    Rewrite with falling factorials using (2): $$\frac{\binom Kj \binom{N-K}{m-j}}{\binom Nm} = \binom mj \frac{(K)_j\,(N-K)_{m-j}}{(N)_m}.$$ There are $j$ factors in $(K)_j$, $m - j$ in $(N-K)_{m-j}$ and $m$ in $(N)_m$. Divide each factor by $N$: $(K - i)/N \to p$, $(N - K - i)/N \to 1 - p$ and $(N - i)/N \to 1$ for each fixed $i$. A product of finitely many convergent factors converges to the product of the limits, giving $\binom mj p^j (1-p)^{m-j}$.
