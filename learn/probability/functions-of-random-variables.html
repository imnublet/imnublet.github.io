C01  /  Lesson 3.4

Functions of random variables

Push a distribution through a function. Sums become convolutions, and smooth maps pick up a Jacobian.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 30 min Y&G ch. 6
After this lesson you can
  • derive the law of $Y = g(X)$ by the CDF method, and with the change-of-variables formula when $g$ is monotone;
  • handle non-monotone $g$ by summing over preimages;
  • prove the convolution formula for sums of independent variables and apply it;
  • find the laws of maxima and minima;
  • use the two-dimensional Jacobian formula, and derive the Box–Muller sampler from it.

§ 01 MethodThe CDF method

Given the law of $X$ (or of $(X, Y)$) and a function $g$, what is the law of $W = g(X)$ or $W = g(X, Y)$? For discrete variables lesson 2.1 already answered it: add up the masses that land on each value. For continuous variables the reliable method is always the same: compute

$$F_W(w) = \P[g(X, Y) \le w] = \iint_{\{(x,y)\, :\, g(x,y) \le w\}} f_{X,Y}(x, y) \d x\d y \tag{1}$$

by drawing the region, then differentiate in $w$. Every formula in this lesson is (1) worked out once and for all for a common case.

§ 02 One variableMonotone and non-monotone maps

Theorem 1monotone change of variables

Let $X$ have density $f_X$, and let $g$ be strictly monotone and differentiable on an open interval containing the support of $X$, with inverse $h = g^{-1}$. Then $Y = g(X)$ has density

$$f_Y(y) = f_X\big(h(y)\big)\,\big|h'(y)\big| \tag{2}$$

for $y$ in the range of $g$, and $0$ elsewhere.

Proof

Increasing $g$. $\{g(X) \le y\} = \{X \le h(y)\}$, so $F_Y(y) = F_X(h(y))$. Differentiate with the chain rule: $f_Y(y) = f_X(h(y))\,h'(y)$, and $h' > 0$.

Decreasing $g$. Now $\{g(X) \le y\} = \{X \ge h(y)\}$, so $F_Y(y) = 1 - F_X(h(y))$ (no atom, since $X$ is continuous), and $f_Y(y) = -f_X(h(y))\,h'(y) = f_X(h(y))\,|h'(y)|$ because $h' < 0$. ∎

Read (2) as conservation of probability: the mass $f_X(x)\,|\d x|$ in a small interval is carried to an interval of length $|\d y| = |g'(x)|\,|\d x|$, so the density is divided by the stretch factor $|g'(x)| = 1/|h'(y)|$. This is the formula that normalising flows evaluate (in $n$ dimensions, with a determinant) to compute exact likelihoods. For an affine map $Y = aX + b$ it gives $f_Y(y) = \frac1{|a|}f_X\big(\frac{y - b}{a}\big)$; with $X$ Gaussian it re-proves lesson 2.4, exercise 1.

If $g$ is not one-to-one, split the line into pieces where it is. When $g$ is differentiable and every $y$ has finitely many preimages $x_1(y), \dots, x_k(y)$, each with $g'(x_i) \ne 0$, the CDF method gives one term per branch:

$$f_Y(y) = \sum_{i} \frac{f_X\big(x_i(y)\big)}{\big|g'\big(x_i(y)\big)\big|}. \tag{3}$$
Examplesquare of a standard normal

$Y = Z^2$, $Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$. For $y > 0$ the preimages are $\pm\sqrt y$ and $|g'(\pm\sqrt y)| = 2\sqrt y$. By (3) and evenness of $\phi$,

$$f_Y(y) = \frac{2\phi(\sqrt y)}{2\sqrt y} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi y}}\,e^{-y/2}, \qquad y > 0.$$

This is the chi-squared law with one degree of freedom. Note the density is unbounded at $0$ but still integrable. The CDF method confirms it without (3): $F_Y(y) = \P[-\sqrt y \le Z \le \sqrt y] = 2\Phi(\sqrt y) - 1$, whose derivative is the same.

Worked example 3.4.1squaring a variable whose range is not symmetric
Problem

$X \sim \text{Uniform}(-1, 2)$ and $Y = X^2$. Find $f_Y$.

Approach

$g(x) = x^2$ is not monotone on $(-1, 2)$, so Theorem 1 does not apply directly. Use the CDF method: write $\{Y \le y\}$ as an event about $X$, compute its probability, then differentiate. The event changes shape at $y = 1$, where the negative branch $-\sqrt y$ leaves the support.

Solution

  1. Range. $Y \in [0, 4)$, so $F_Y(y) = 0$ for $y < 0$ and $1$ for $y \ge 4$.

  2. Case $0 \le y < 1$. $\{Y \le y\} = \{-\sqrt y \le X \le \sqrt y\}$, an interval of length $2\sqrt y$ inside $(-1, 2)$, so $F_Y(y) = \frac{2\sqrt y}{3}$.

  3. Case $1 \le y < 4$. Now $-\sqrt y \le -1$, so the event is $\{-1 < X \le \sqrt y\}$, of length $\sqrt y + 1$: $F_Y(y) = \frac{\sqrt y + 1}{3}$. Both formulas give $\frac23$ at $y = 1$ ✓, and the second gives $1$ at $y = 4$ ✓.

  4. Differentiate.

    $$f_Y(y) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{1}{3\sqrt y}, & 0 < y < 1, \\[1ex] \dfrac{1}{6\sqrt y}, & 1 < y < 4, \\[1ex] 0, & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$ Check: $\int_0^1 \frac{\d y}{3\sqrt y} = \frac23$ and $\int_1^4 \frac{\d y}{6\sqrt y} = \frac{2(2 - 1)}{6} = \frac13$, total $1$ ✓.
Result

The density halves at $y = 1$: below $1$ each $y$ has two preimages $\pm\sqrt y$ in the support, above it only one. This is what (3) predicts, but the CDF method found it without having to track the preimages separately.

§ 03 SumsConvolution

Theorem 2density of a sum

If $X, Y$ are jointly continuous, $W = X + Y$ has density $f_W(w) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_{X,Y}(x, w - x) \d x$. If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, this is the convolution

$$f_W(w) = (f_X * f_Y)(w) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f_X(x)\, f_Y(w - x) \d x. \tag{4}$$

For independent integer-valued $X, Y$, likewise $P_W(w) = \sum_k P_X(k)\,P_Y(w - k)$.

Proof

By (1), integrating over the half-plane $\{x + y \le w\}$ with $x$ outermost, then substituting $v = x + y$ in the inner integral:

$$F_W(w) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^{w - x} f_{X,Y}(x, y)\d y \d x = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \int_{-\infty}^{w} f_{X,Y}(x, v - x)\d v \d x = \int_{-\infty}^{w}\Big(\int_{-\infty}^\infty f_{X,Y}(x, v - x)\d x\Big)\d v,$$

exchanging the order by Tonelli. So $F_W$ is the integral of the bracket, which is therefore a density of $W$. With independence, $f_{X,Y}(x, v - x) = f_X(x)f_Y(v - x)$. The discrete case: $\{W = w\}$ is the disjoint union over $k$ of $\{X = k, Y = w - k\}$. ∎

Exampletwo exponential waiting times

$X, Y \iid \text{Exp}(\lambda)$. For $w \ge 0$ the integrand of (4) is non-zero only for $0 \le x \le w$, so

$$f_W(w) = \int_0^w \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,\lambda e^{-\lambda(w - x)}\d x = \lambda^2 e^{-\lambda w}\int_0^w \d x = \lambda^2\,w\,e^{-\lambda w}.$$

This is the Erlang law of order 2: the time until the second event of a Poisson process (lesson 6.2). The $e^{-\lambda x}$ factors combine into one exponential because the exponent is linear, and that is what makes exponential and Gaussian convolutions computable in closed form.

Convolution smooths: two uniforms on $(0,1)$ convolve to a triangle on $(0,2)$, three to a piecewise quadratic, and the shapes approach a Gaussian quickly. The demo shows this; the reason is the central limit theorem (lesson 4.6), and the efficient way to compute repeated convolutions is through transforms, which turn $*$ into multiplication (lesson 4.5, and the Fourier transform in the signal-processing course).

Worked example 3.4.2the sum of two uniforms, with careful limits
Problem

$X, Y \iid \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$ and $W = X + Y$. Find $f_W$ and $\P[W \le 1.5]$.

Approach

Use the convolution (4). The integrand is a product of two indicators, so the whole difficulty is to find, for each $w$, the set of $x$ where both are $1$. Write both constraints on $x$, intersect, and split into cases where the intersection changes form.

Solution

  1. Integrand. $f_X(x) f_Y(w - x) = \ind\{0 < x < 1\}\,\ind\{0 < w - x < 1\} = \ind\{0 < x < 1\}\,\ind\{w - 1 < x < w\}$.

  2. Intersect. Both hold iff $\max(0, w - 1) < x < \min(1, w)$, so $f_W(w) = \big(\min(1, w) - \max(0, w - 1)\big)^+$.

  3. Cases. For $0 \le w \le 1$: $\min = w$, $\max = 0$, so $f_W(w) = w$. For $1 < w \le 2$: $\min = 1$, $\max = w - 1$, so $f_W(w) = 2 - w$. Outside $[0, 2]$, $f_W = 0$.

  4. Probability. Use the complement, a small triangle: $\P[W > 1.5] = \int_{1.5}^2 (2 - w)\d w = \frac{(0.5)^2}{2} = 0.125$, so $\P[W \le 1.5] = 0.875$.

Result

$f_W$ is the triangle $1 - |w - 1|$ on $[0, 2]$, and $\P[W \le 1.5] = \frac78$. Summing two flat densities gives a tent; summing more makes the shape smoother and more bell-like, the central limit theorem at work.

§ 04 ExtremesMaxima and minima

Theorem 3max and min of independent variables

If $X_1, \dots, X_n$ are independent with CDFs $F_i$, then

$$F_{\max_i X_i}(w) = \prod_{i=1}^n F_i(w), \qquad \P\big[\min_i X_i > w\big] = \prod_{i=1}^n \big(1 - F_i(w)\big). \tag{5}$$
Proof

$\{\max_i X_i \le w\} = \bigcap_i \{X_i \le w\}$ and $\{\min_i X_i > w\} = \bigcap_i \{X_i > w\}$; independence makes the probabilities multiply. ∎

For $n$ iid Uniform$(0,1)$ variables, $F_{\max}(w) = w^n$ and $f_{\max}(w) = nw^{n-1}$ on $(0,1)$. A series system fails at the minimum of its components' lifetimes and a parallel system at the maximum, so (5) is the continuous-time version of the reliability formulas in lesson 1.4.

§ 05 Two variablesThe Jacobian formula and Box–Muller

Theorem 4change of variables in the plane

Let $(X, Y)$ have density $f_{X,Y}$ supported in an open set $D$, and let $T : D \to E$ be a bijection onto an open set $E$, continuously differentiable with continuously differentiable inverse $H = T^{-1}$. Then $(V, W) = T(X, Y)$ has density

$$f_{V,W}(v, w) = f_{X,Y}\big(H(v, w)\big)\,\big|\det J_H(v, w)\big|, \qquad (v, w) \in E, \tag{6}$$

where $J_H$ is the $2 \times 2$ matrix of partial derivatives of $H$.

Proof

For any region $B \subseteq E$, $\P[(V, W) \in B] = \P[(X, Y) \in H(B)] = \iint_{H(B)} f_{X,Y}(x, y)\d x\d y$. The multivariable change-of-variables theorem of calculus, with the substitution $(x, y) = H(v, w)$, rewrites this as $\iint_B f_{X,Y}(H(v,w))\,|\det J_H(v,w)|\d v\d w$. Since this holds for every region $B$, the integrand is a density of $(V, W)$. ∎

Formula (6) is (2) with $|h'|$ replaced by the factor by which $H$ scales areas. The standard application turns two independent normals into polar coordinates.

Theorem 5polar form of two independent normals

Let $X, Y \iid \mathcal N(0,1)$ and write $(X, Y) = (R\cos\Theta, R\sin\Theta)$ with $R > 0$, $\Theta \in [0, 2\pi)$. Then $R$ and $\Theta$ are independent, $\Theta \sim \text{Uniform}[0, 2\pi)$, and $R^2 \sim \text{Exp}(\tfrac12)$. Conversely, if $U_1, U_2 \iid \text{Uniform}(0,1)$,

$$X = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\,\cos(2\pi U_2), \qquad Y = \sqrt{-2\ln U_1}\,\sin(2\pi U_2) \tag{7}$$

are independent standard normals (the Box–Muller transform).

Proof

The joint density is $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac1{2\pi}e^{-(x^2 + y^2)/2}$ (lesson 3.2, Theorem 2). The inverse map $H(r, \theta) = (r\cos\theta, r\sin\theta)$ from $(0, \infty) \times (0, 2\pi)$ onto the plane minus a ray (a set of probability zero) has

$$\det J_H = \det\begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -r\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & r\cos\theta \end{pmatrix} = r(\cos^2\theta + \sin^2\theta) = r.$$

By (6), $f_{R,\Theta}(r, \theta) = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-r^2/2}\,r = \big(\tfrac{1}{2\pi}\big)\big(re^{-r^2/2}\big)$ on $(0,\infty) \times (0, 2\pi)$. This is a product over a product set, so by lesson 3.2, Theorem 3, $R$ and $\Theta$ are independent, $\Theta$ is uniform, and $f_R(r) = re^{-r^2/2}$. Then $\P[R^2 > s] = \P[R > \sqrt s] = \int_{\sqrt s}^\infty re^{-r^2/2}\d r = e^{-s/2}$: $R^2 \sim \text{Exp}(\tfrac12)$.

For the converse, by inverse-transform sampling (lesson 2.3) $-2\ln U_1 \sim \text{Exp}(\tfrac12)$ (because $1 - U_1$ is uniform too) and $2\pi U_2$ is uniform on $(0, 2\pi)$, independently. So $(\sqrt{-2\ln U_1}, 2\pi U_2)$ has the law of $(R, \Theta)$, and mapping it through $H$ gives a pair with the law of $(X, Y)$. ∎

This is the sampler inside every demo on these pages: Learn.randn in learn.js computes $\sqrt{-2\ln(1 - u)}\cos(2\pi u')$, the first coordinate of (7). The polar substitution in the proof of the Gaussian integral (lesson 2.4) is the same change of variables, carried out before the normalising constant was known.

Worked example 3.4.3the product of two uniforms by a Jacobian
Problem

$X, Y \iid \text{Uniform}(0, 1)$. Find the density of $V = XY$.

Approach

Theorem 4 maps pairs to pairs, so complete $V$ with an auxiliary variable, $W = X$, that makes the map invertible and the inverse easy. Compute the joint density of $(V, W)$ by (6), then integrate $W$ out. The support $E$ must be worked out carefully, because it sets the limits of that integral.

Solution

  1. Map and inverse. $T(x, y) = (xy, x)$ on $D = (0, 1)^2$. Given $(v, w)$: $x = w$, $y = v/w$, so $H(v, w) = (w, v/w)$. The image is $E = \{0 < v < w < 1\}$, since $0 < y < 1 \iff 0 < v < w$.

  2. Jacobian.

    $$J_H = \begin{pmatrix} \partial x/\partial v & \partial x/\partial w \\ \partial y/\partial v & \partial y/\partial w \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1/w & -v/w^2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\det J_H| = \frac1w.$$

  3. Joint density. By (6), $f_{V,W}(v, w) = f_{X,Y}(w, v/w) \cdot \frac1w = \frac1w$ on $E$.

  4. Marginalise. For $0 < v < 1$, $w$ ranges over $(v, 1)$: $f_V(v) = \int_v^1 \frac{\d w}{w} = -\ln v$.

  5. Check. $\int_0^1 -\ln v \d v = 1$ ✓, and $\E[V] = \int_0^1 -v\ln v \d v = \frac14 = \E[X]\E[Y]$ ✓ (Theorem 4 of lesson 3.2).

Result

$f_V(v) = -\ln v$ on $(0, 1)$: unbounded at $0$, because a product of two numbers in $(0,1)$ is pulled towards $0$. The steps are the same for any pair transformation: complete the map, invert, take $|\det J_H|$, find the image set, integrate out the auxiliary variable.

§ 06 In codeChecking derived distributions

§ 07 PracticeExercises

  1. Show that if $U \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$ then $-\ln U / \lambda \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, using (2).core

    Solution

    $g(u) = -\ln u/\lambda$ is decreasing on $(0,1)$ with inverse $h(y) = e^{-\lambda y}$, $|h'(y)| = \lambda e^{-\lambda y}$, and $f_U(h(y)) = 1$ for $y > 0$. So $f_Y(y) = \lambda e^{-\lambda y}$.

  2. Show that the sum of independent Poisson$(\alpha)$ and Poisson$(\beta)$ variables is Poisson$(\alpha + \beta)$.core

    Solution

    By the discrete convolution, for $w \ge 0$, $$P_W(w) = \sum_{k=0}^w \frac{\alpha^k e^{-\alpha}}{k!}\frac{\beta^{w-k}e^{-\beta}}{(w-k)!} = \frac{e^{-(\alpha+\beta)}}{w!}\sum_{k=0}^w\binom wk \alpha^k\beta^{w-k} = \frac{(\alpha+\beta)^w e^{-(\alpha+\beta)}}{w!}$$ by the binomial theorem.

  3. For $n$ iid Uniform$(0,1)$ variables, find $\E[\max_i U_i]$ and $\E[\min_i U_i]$.core

    Solution

    $\E[\max] = \int_0^1 w\,nw^{n-1}\d w = \frac{n}{n+1}$. By symmetry ($1 - U_i$ are also iid uniform and $\min_i U_i = 1 - \max_i(1 - U_i)$), $\E[\min] = \frac{1}{n+1}$. The $n$ points split $[0,1]$ into $n + 1$ gaps of equal expected length.

  4. Use (4) to show that if $X, Y \iid \mathcal N(0, 1)$ then $X + Y \sim \mathcal N(0, 2)$.core

    Solution

    $f_W(w) = \frac1{2\pi}\int e^{-x^2/2 - (w - x)^2/2}\d x$. Complete the square: $x^2/2 + (w - x)^2/2 = (x - w/2)^2 + w^2/4$. So $f_W(w) = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-w^2/4}\int e^{-(x - w/2)^2}\d x = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-w^2/4}\sqrt\pi = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \cdot 2}}e^{-w^2/(2\cdot2)}$, the $\mathcal N(0,2)$ density. Lesson 3.6 does all linear combinations at once.

  5. Let $X, Y \iid \text{Exp}(1)$. Show that $V = X + Y$ and $W = X/(X + Y)$ are independent, with $W \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)$.stretch

    Solution

    The inverse map is $H(v, w) = (vw, v(1 - w))$ on $(0, \infty) \times (0,1)$, with $\det J_H = \det\begin{pmatrix} w & v \\ 1 - w & -v\end{pmatrix} = -vw - v(1-w) = -v$. By (6), $f_{V,W}(v, w) = e^{-vw}e^{-v(1-w)}\,v = v e^{-v}$ on $(0,\infty) \times (0,1)$: a product of $ve^{-v}$ (Erlang 2, as in the example) and $1$ on a product set. Given the total waiting time, the split point is uniform.
