Discrete random variables and PMFs

A random variable is a number computed from the outcome. Its PMF is all we need to know about it on its own.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 25 min Y&G ch. 3
After this lesson you can
  • define a random variable as a function on $\Omega$ and say what makes it discrete;
  • prove the defining properties of a PMF and compute $\P[X \in B]$ from it;
  • derive the Bernoulli, geometric, binomial, Pascal, discrete uniform and Poisson PMFs from experiments;
  • prove that the binomial law tends to the Poisson law when $n \to \infty$ with $np$ fixed;
  • find the PMF of $Y = g(X)$.

§ 01 DefinitionNumbers computed from outcomes

Module 1 worked with events. Most questions, though, are about quantities: the number of errors in a packet, the time until a failure, the reading on a sensor. A random variable is such a quantity, and formally it is nothing more than a rule that reads a number off each outcome.

Definitionrandom variable

A random variable is a function $X : \Omega \to \R$ such that $\{X \le x\} = \{\omega \in \Omega : X(\omega) \le x\}$ is an event for every $x \in \R$. Its range is $S_X = \{X(\omega) : \omega \in \Omega\}$. $X$ is discrete if $S_X$ is countable (finite or countably infinite).

The measurability condition guarantees that every question of the form "is $X$ in $B$?" for a reasonable set $B$ is an event and so has a probability. On a countable $\Omega$ with all subsets as events it holds automatically, and you may forget it. The notation $\{X \in B\}$ is shorthand for the event $\{\omega : X(\omega) \in B\}$, the preimage $X^{-1}(B)$.

Example: toss a coin three times, $\Omega = \{H,T\}^3$, and let $X(\omega)$ be the number of heads. Then $S_X = \{0,1,2,3\}$ and, for instance, $\{X = 2\} = \{HHT, HTH, THH\}$. The random variable is the counting map $f$ from lesson 1.1, now with a name.

§ 02 DistributionThe probability mass function

DefinitionPMF

The probability mass function of a discrete random variable $X$ is $P_X(x) = \P[X = x]$ for $x \in \R$.

Theorem 1properties of a PMF

For a discrete random variable $X$:

  1. $P_X(x) \ge 0$ for all $x$, and $P_X(x) = 0$ for $x \notin S_X$;
  2. $\sum_{x \in S_X} P_X(x) = 1$;
  3. for any $B \subseteq \R$, $\displaystyle \P[X \in B] = \sum_{x \in B \cap S_X} P_X(x)$.
Proof

(a) is non-negativity, plus $\{X = x\} = \varnothing$ when $x \notin S_X$. For (b) and (c), the events $\{X = x\}$, $x \in S_X$, are pairwise disjoint (a function takes one value at each $\omega$) and their union is $\Omega$ (it takes some value), so they form a countable partition of $\Omega$. By the decomposition theorem of lesson 1.1, $\{X \in B\}$ is the disjoint union of $\{X \in B\} \cap \{X = x\}$ over $x \in S_X$, and that intersection is $\{X = x\}$ if $x \in B$ and empty otherwise. Countable additivity gives (c), and (c) with $B = \R$ gives (b). ∎

Part (c) is the important one: once we know $P_X$ we can answer every question about $X$ alone without going back to $\Omega$. The distribution of $X$, the map $B \mapsto \P[X \in B]$, is a probability measure on $\R$, and for discrete $X$ the PMF determines it. Two random variables on very different sample spaces can have the same PMF, and then they are indistinguishable by any question that involves only one of them.

Worked example 2.1.1the PMF of a gap between two dice
Problem

Roll two fair dice and let $X$ be the absolute difference of the two faces. Find the PMF of $X$ and $\P[X \ge 3]$.

Approach

A random variable is a function on $\Omega$, so its PMF is found by grouping outcomes by the value of $X$ and counting. Then use Theorem 1(c) for the event.

Solution

  1. Range. $\Omega = \{1, \dots, 6\}^2$ with $36$ equally likely outcomes and $X(\omega_1, \omega_2) = |\omega_1 - \omega_2|$, so $S_X = \{0, 1, \dots, 5\}$.

  2. Count each level set. For $x = 0$ the outcomes are the $6$ doubles. For $x \ge 1$, the ordered pairs with $\omega_1 - \omega_2 = x$ are $(x + 1, 1), \dots, (6, 6 - x)$, which is $6 - x$ pairs, and the same number have $\omega_2 - \omega_1 = x$. So $$P_X(0) = \frac{6}{36}, \qquad P_X(x) = \frac{2(6 - x)}{36}, \quad x = 1, \dots, 5,$$ i.e. $\frac{6}{36}, \frac{10}{36}, \frac{8}{36}, \frac{6}{36}, \frac{4}{36}, \frac{2}{36}$.

  3. Check normalisation. $6 + 10 + 8 + 6 + 4 + 2 = 36$. ✓ (Theorem 1(b).)

  4. The event. By Theorem 1(c), $\P[X \ge 3] = P_X(3) + P_X(4) + P_X(5) = \frac{6 + 4 + 2}{36} = \frac13$.

Result

$P_X(0) = \frac16$, $P_X(x) = \frac{6 - x}{18}$ for $x = 1, \dots, 5$, and $\P[X \ge 3] = \frac13$. Every discrete PMF from an equally likely experiment is found this way: partition $\Omega$ by the value of $X$, count each block, check the counts add to $|\Omega|$.

§ 03 FamiliesThe standard discrete laws

Each family below is derived from an experiment, which is the best way to remember both the formula and when it applies. In all of them, $0 < p < 1$ and the trials are independent Bernoulli$(p)$ trials as in lesson 1.4.

DefinitionBernoulli and binomial

$X \sim \text{Bernoulli}(p)$ if $P_X(1) = p$, $P_X(0) = 1 - p$: the indicator of success in one trial. $K \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$ if $P_K(k) = \binom nk p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$ for $k = 0, \dots, n$: the number of successes in $n$ trials (lesson 1.4, Theorem 3).

Theorem 2geometric and Pascal laws

Let $L_k$ be the index of the trial on which the $k$-th success occurs. Then

$$P_{L_k}(\ell) = \binom{\ell - 1}{k - 1} p^k (1-p)^{\ell - k}, \qquad \ell = k, k+1, \dots \tag{1}$$

(the Pascal or negative binomial law). For $k = 1$ this is the geometric law $P_{L_1}(\ell) = p(1-p)^{\ell - 1}$, $\ell \ge 1$.

Proof

$\{L_k = \ell\}$ is the intersection of two events: $A$ = "exactly $k-1$ successes in trials $1, \dots, \ell - 1$" and $B$ = "trial $\ell$ is a success". They depend on disjoint sets of trials, so they are independent (lesson 1.4, Theorem 2). By the binomial law $\P[A] = \binom{\ell-1}{k-1}p^{k-1}(1-p)^{\ell-k}$, and $\P[B] = p$. Multiply. ∎

Beware the second convention: some texts call "number of failures before the first success" geometric, which is $L_1 - 1$ with PMF $p(1-p)^j$, $j \ge 0$. Both are in common use (NumPy uses ours, SciPy's geom too, but nbinom counts failures). Check before using a library.

Definitiondiscrete uniform and Poisson

$X \sim \text{Uniform}\{k, \dots, l\}$ if $P_X(x) = 1/(l - k + 1)$ for each integer $x \in [k, l]$. And $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\alpha)$, $\alpha > 0$, if

$$P_X(x) = \frac{\alpha^x e^{-\alpha}}{x!}, \qquad x = 0, 1, 2, \dots \tag{2}$$

This sums to one because $\sum_{x \ge 0} \alpha^x / x! = e^{\alpha}$. The Poisson law counts occurrences of rare, independent events in a fixed window (photons on a detector, requests to a server, typos on a page), and the reason is the following limit.

Theorem 3Poisson limit of the binomial

Fix $\alpha > 0$ and $x \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$. If $K_n \sim \text{Binomial}(n, \alpha/n)$, then

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} \P[K_n = x] = \frac{\alpha^x e^{-\alpha}}{x!}. \tag{3}$$
Proof

For $n \ge x$ and $n > \alpha$, write the binomial probability with $p = \alpha/n$ and regroup:

$$\binom nx \Big(\frac\alpha n\Big)^x \Big(1 - \frac\alpha n\Big)^{n-x} = \frac{\alpha^x}{x!} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{n(n-1)\cdots(n-x+1)}{n^x}}_{(i)} \cdot \underbrace{\Big(1 - \frac\alpha n\Big)^{n}}_{(ii)} \cdot \underbrace{\Big(1 - \frac\alpha n\Big)^{-x}}_{(iii)}.$$

(i) is a product of $x$ factors $(n - j)/n = 1 - j/n$, each tending to $1$, and $x$ is fixed, so (i) $\to 1$. (iii) is a fixed power of a quantity tending to $1$, so (iii) $\to 1$. For (ii), $\log(1 - \alpha/n)^n = n \log(1 - \alpha/n)$. Since $\log(1 - t) = -t + r(t)$ with $|r(t)| \le t^2$ for $0 \le t \le \tfrac12$ (Taylor with remainder), for $n \ge 2\alpha$ we get $n\log(1 - \alpha/n) = -\alpha + n\,r(\alpha/n)$ with $|n\,r(\alpha/n)| \le \alpha^2/n \to 0$. By continuity of $\exp$, (ii) $\to e^{-\alpha}$. The product of the limits is (3). ∎

Pointwise convergence is the weakest useful statement. A much stronger, quantitative one (Le Cam's inequality) says the total variation distance $\tfrac12 \sum_x |\P[K_n = x] - P_X(x)|$ between Binomial$(n, p)$ and Poisson$(np)$ is at most $np^2$, which here is $\alpha^2 / n$. The demo shows both.

Worked example 2.1.2choosing the right family
Problem

Identify the law and compute the probability. (a) A server receives on average $3$ requests per second, from many independent users each of whom rarely sends one. Find $\P[\text{more than } 5 \text{ requests in a second}]$. (b) A penetration test succeeds on each independent attempt with probability $0.3$. Find the probability that the second success comes on attempt $5$.

Approach

Match the description to a mechanism, not to keywords. "Many independent rare events, known mean" is the Poisson limit of the binomial (Theorem 3). "Index of the $k$-th success in Bernoulli trials" is the Pascal law (Theorem 2).

Solution

  1. (a) Model. Each of $n$ users sends a request in a given second with small probability $\alpha/n$; by Theorem 3 the count $X$ is approximately Poisson$(\alpha)$. The Poisson$(\alpha)$ law has mean $\alpha$ (lesson 2.2), so the stated average gives $\alpha = 3$.

  2. (a) Complement. "More than 5" has infinitely many terms, so use the complement: $$\P[X > 5] = 1 - \sum_{x=0}^{5} \frac{3^x e^{-3}}{x!} = 1 - e^{-3}\Big(1 + 3 + \frac92 + \frac{27}{6} + \frac{81}{24} + \frac{243}{120}\Big) = 1 - 18.4\,e^{-3} \approx 0.0839.$$

  3. (b) Model. $L_2$ = index of the second success, with $p = 0.3$. By (1) with $k = 2$, $\P[L_2 = \ell] = (\ell - 1)\,p^2 (1-p)^{\ell - 2}$.

  4. (b) Evaluate. $\P[L_2 = 5] = 4 \cdot (0.3)^2 (0.7)^3 = 4 \cdot 0.09 \cdot 0.343 = 0.12348$. The factor $4 = \binom41$ counts where the first success sits among the first four attempts.

Result

(a) $\approx 0.084$; (b) $\approx 0.123$. Most discrete problems go this way: decide which mechanism generated the count, then evaluate the PMF, using the complement when the event has infinitely many terms.

§ 04 TransformationsFunctions of a random variable

If $X$ is a random variable and $g : \R \to \R$, then $Y = g(X)$, the composition $\omega \mapsto g(X(\omega))$, is again a random variable (for discrete $X$ there is nothing to check).

Theorem 4PMF of a function

If $X$ is discrete and $Y = g(X)$, then $S_Y = g(S_X)$ is countable and

$$P_Y(y) = \sum_{x \in S_X :\, g(x) = y} P_X(x). \tag{4}$$
Proof

$\{Y = y\} = \{X \in g^{-1}(\{y\})\}$; apply Theorem 1(c) with $B = g^{-1}(\{y\}) = \{x : g(x) = y\}$. ∎

When $g$ is one-to-one on $S_X$, (4) has a single term: the masses are just relabelled. When it is not, masses that land on the same $y$ add. Example: $X$ uniform on $\{-2, -1, 0, 1, 2\}$ and $Y = X^2$ give $P_Y(0) = \tfrac15$, $P_Y(1) = P_Y(4) = \tfrac25$.

§ 06 PracticeExercises

  1. Show that the geometric law is memoryless: $\P[L_1 > m + n \mid L_1 > m] = \P[L_1 > n]$ for all integers $m, n \ge 0$.core

    Solution

    $\{L_1 > n\}$ is "the first $n$ trials fail", so $\P[L_1 > n] = (1-p)^n$. Since $\{L_1 > m+n\} \subseteq \{L_1 > m\}$, $\P[L_1 > m+n \mid L_1 > m] = (1-p)^{m+n} / (1-p)^m = (1-p)^n$.

  2. For $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\alpha)$, find $\P[X \text{ is even}]$.core

    Solution

    $\sum_{x \text{ even}} \alpha^x/x! = \tfrac12(e^\alpha + e^{-\alpha})$, since adding the series for $e^{\alpha}$ and $e^{-\alpha}$ doubles the even terms and cancels the odd ones. Multiplying by $e^{-\alpha}$, $\P[X \text{ even}] = \tfrac12(1 + e^{-2\alpha})$, which is always above $\tfrac12$.

  3. Show that the Poisson PMF (2) increases while $x < \alpha$ and decreases after, so its mode is $\lfloor \alpha \rfloor$ (and also $\alpha - 1$ if $\alpha$ is an integer).core

    Solution

    $P_X(x)/P_X(x-1) = \alpha/x$, which is $> 1$ iff $x < \alpha$ and $= 1$ iff $x = \alpha$. Compare exercise 6 of lesson 1.4: this is its $n \to \infty$ limit.

  4. Let $X \sim \text{Geometric}(p)$ (our convention) and $Y = X \bmod 2$. Find $P_Y$.core

    Solution

    By (4), $P_Y(1) = \sum_{j \ge 0} p(1-p)^{2j} = \frac{p}{1 - (1-p)^2} = \frac{1}{2 - p}$, and $P_Y(0) = \frac{1-p}{2-p}$. For $p = \tfrac12$ this gives $\tfrac23$ and $\tfrac13$, matching exercise 3 of lesson 1.2.

  5. Each of $n$ independent requests hits a cache with probability $1 - \alpha/n$. Use Theorem 3 to approximate the probability of at most two misses when $n = 1000$, $\alpha = 2$, and use Le Cam's bound to say how far off the approximation can be.stretch

    Solution

    The misses are Binomial$(1000, 0.002)$. The Poisson$(2)$ approximation gives $e^{-2}(1 + 2 + 2) = 5e^{-2} \approx 0.6767$. For any event $B$, $|\P[K \in B] - \P[X \in B]|$ is at most the total variation distance (it is the maximum over $B$), here at most $np^2 = 0.004$. So the exact answer lies in $[0.672, 0.681]$; it is in fact $\approx 0.6767$.
