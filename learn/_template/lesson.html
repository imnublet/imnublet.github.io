The sample mean and the law of large numbers

Averages of noisy measurements settle down. Here is exactly how fast, and the two-line proof of why.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 15 min Y&G § 10.1–10.3
After this lesson you can
  • compute the mean and variance of the sample mean $M_n(X)$;
  • state and prove the weak law of large numbers from Chebyshev's inequality;
  • size an experiment so an average is within $\varepsilon$ of the truth with a given confidence.

§ 01 SetupThe sample mean

Repeat an experiment $n$ times and record a measurement each time. We model the record as random variables $X_1, \dots, X_n \iid X$ with $\E[X] = \mu_X$ and $\Var[X] = \sigma_X^2 < \infty$.

Definitionsample mean

The sample mean of $n$ independent trials of $X$ is the random variable

$$M_n(X) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i. \tag{1}$$

$M_n(X)$ is itself a random variable: run the $n$ trials again and you get a different number. Its first two moments follow from linearity of expectation and from independence.

Theorem 1moments of the sample mean

$$\E[M_n(X)] = \mu_X, \qquad \Var[M_n(X)] = \frac{\sigma_X^2}{n}. \tag{2}$$

Proof

Linearity gives $\E[M_n(X)] = \tfrac1n \sum_i \E[X_i] = \tfrac1n \cdot n\mu_X = \mu_X$. For the variance, expand the variance of a sum into covariances:

$$\Var\Big[\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\Big] = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n \Cov[X_i, X_j] = \sum_{i=1}^n \Var[X_i] + \sum_{i \ne j} \Cov[X_i, X_j].$$

Independence implies $\Cov[X_i, X_j] = 0$ for $i \ne j$, so the sum is $n\sigma_X^2$. Scaling by $1/n$ scales the variance by $1/n^2$, giving $\sigma_X^2 / n$. ∎

So $M_n(X)$ is an unbiased estimator of $\mu_X$, and its standard deviation shrinks like $\sigma_X / \sqrt{n}$. To halve the error bar you need four times the data.

§ 02 ConvergenceThe weak law of large numbers

Variance going to zero should mean the distribution of $M_n(X)$ collapses onto $\mu_X$. Chebyshev's inequality, $\P[|Y - \mu_Y| \ge c] \le \Var[Y]/c^2$, turns that intuition into a bound.

Theorem 2weak law of large numbers

For any $c > 0$,

$$\P\big[\,|M_n(X) - \mu_X| \ge c\,\big] \le \frac{\sigma_X^2}{n c^2} \xrightarrow[n \to \infty]{} 0. \tag{3}$$
Proof

Apply Chebyshev to $Y = M_n(X)$, using $\mu_Y = \mu_X$ and $\Var[Y] = \sigma_X^2/n$ from Theorem 1. ∎

Note

The proof only used $\Cov[X_i, X_j] = 0$. Pairwise uncorrelated trials with common mean and variance are enough; full independence is not needed.

§ 03 In codeChecking the $1/n$ law

Estimate $\Var[M_n]$ empirically across many repetitions and compare with $\sigma_X^2/n$.

§ 04 PracticeExercises

  1. Let $X_1, \dots, X_n$ have common variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho = \Cov[X_i,X_j]/\sigma^2$ for $i \ne j$. Compute $\Var[M_n]$. What happens as $n \to \infty$ when $\rho > 0$?core

    Solution

    There are $n$ diagonal terms and $n(n-1)$ off-diagonal terms, so $$\Var[M_n] = \frac{1}{n^2}\big(n\sigma^2 + n(n-1)\rho\sigma^2\big) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n} + \frac{n-1}{n}\rho\sigma^2 \;\longrightarrow\; \rho\sigma^2.$$ Positive correlation puts a floor under the error that more samples cannot remove.

  2. A poll estimates a proportion $p$ by $M_n$ of Bernoulli$(p)$ trials. Using only Chebyshev, how large must $n$ be to guarantee $\P[|M_n - p| \ge 0.05] \le 0.05$ for every $p$?core

    Solution

    $\sigma_X^2 = p(1-p) \le 1/4$, so (3) gives the bound $1/(4 n c^2)$. Requiring $1/(4n \cdot 0.05^2) \le 0.05$ gives $n \ge 2000$. The CLT suggests about $385$ is enough: Chebyshev is distribution-free and therefore loose.
