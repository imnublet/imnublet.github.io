Joint CDFs, PMFs and PDFs

Two random variables measured on the same run need one distribution for the pair, not two separate ones.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 25 min Y&G ch. 5
After this lesson you can
  • define the joint CDF of a pair and prove the rectangle formula;
  • explain why monotonicity in each argument is not enough to be a joint CDF;
  • compute probabilities of regions in the plane from a joint PMF or joint density;
  • evaluate $\E[g(X,Y)]$ from the joint law.

§ 01 WhyOne experiment, two numbers

A transmitted bit and a received voltage, the height and weight of a person, a feature vector and its label: most interesting questions involve several random variables defined on the same sample space. Their separate distributions cannot answer questions such as "how often is $Y$ larger than $X$?", because that depends on how the two vary together. We need the distribution of the pair $(X, Y) : \Omega \to \R^2$.

§ 02 DefinitionThe joint CDF

Definitionjoint CDF

For random variables $X, Y$ on the same probability space, the joint CDF is

$$F_{X,Y}(x, y) = \P[X \le x, Y \le y], \qquad (x, y) \in \R^2, \tag{1}$$

where the comma means intersection: $\{X \le x\} \cap \{Y \le y\}$.

Theorem 1properties of a joint CDF
  1. $F_{X,Y}$ is non-decreasing in each argument and right-continuous in each argument;
  2. $F_{X,Y}(x, y) \to 0$ if $x \to -\infty$ or $y \to -\infty$, and $F_{X,Y}(x, y) \to 1$ as $x, y \to \infty$ together;
  3. marginals: $\lim_{y \to \infty} F_{X,Y}(x, y) = F_X(x)$ and $\lim_{x \to \infty} F_{X,Y}(x, y) = F_Y(y)$;
  4. rectangle formula: for $x_1 < x_2$ and $y_1 < y_2$, $$\P[x_1 < X \le x_2,\; y_1 < Y \le y_2] = F(x_2, y_2) - F(x_1, y_2) - F(x_2, y_1) + F(x_1, y_1) \ge 0. \tag{2}$$
Proof

(a) If $x \le x'$ then $\{X \le x, Y \le y\} \subseteq \{X \le x', Y \le y\}$; use monotonicity of $\P$. Right-continuity follows as in lesson 2.3: $\{X \le x + 1/n, Y \le y\} \downarrow \{X \le x, Y \le y\}$, and continuity of probability applies.

(b) $\{X \le -n, Y \le y\} \subseteq \{X \le -n\}$, whose probability tends to $0$. And $\{X \le n, Y \le n\} \uparrow \Omega$, so its probability tends to $1$.

(c) $\{X \le x, Y \le n\} \uparrow \{X \le x\} \cap \Omega = \{X \le x\}$; continuity of probability gives $F_X(x)$.

(d) Let $A = \{X \le x_2, Y \le y_2\}$, $B_1 = \{X \le x_1, Y \le y_2\}$ and $B_2 = \{X \le x_2, Y \le y_1\}$. Then $B_1, B_2 \subseteq A$ and the rectangle event is $A \setminus (B_1 \cup B_2)$, so its probability is $\P[A] - \P[B_1 \cup B_2] = \P[A] - \P[B_1] - \P[B_2] + \P[B_1 \cap B_2]$ by inclusion–exclusion. Finally $B_1 \cap B_2 = \{X \le x_1, Y \le y_1\}$. ∎

Property (d) is not a consequence of (a)–(c). A function can be non-decreasing in each variable, right-continuous and have the right limits, yet assign negative "probability" to a rectangle (exercise 1). So the rectangle inequality, sometimes called being 2-increasing, is part of what it means to be a joint CDF. Conversely, every function with (a), (b) and (2) is the joint CDF of some pair, and the joint CDF determines $\P[(X, Y) \in B]$ for every Borel set $B \subseteq \R^2$, because rectangles generate the Borel sets.

Worked example 3.1.1a rectangle probability from a joint CDF
Problem

$X$ and $Y$ have joint CDF $F(x, y) = (1 - e^{-x})(1 - e^{-2y})$ for $x, y \ge 0$, and $F = 0$ otherwise. Find $\P[1 < X \le 2,\; 0 < Y \le 1]$ and $\P[X > 1, Y > 1]$.

Approach

Rectangle events go straight into the rectangle formula (2). An "upper-right quadrant" is a rectangle with infinite sides: let $x_2, y_2 \to \infty$ and use the limits in Theorem 1.

Solution

  1. Corner values. $F(2, 1) = (1 - e^{-2})(1 - e^{-2})$, $F(1, 1) = (1 - e^{-1})(1 - e^{-2})$, and $F(2, 0) = F(1, 0) = 0$ because $1 - e^{0} = 0$.

  2. Rectangle formula. By (2), $$\P[1 < X \le 2,\; 0 < Y \le 1] = F(2,1) - F(1,1) - F(2,0) + F(1,0) = (1 - e^{-2})\big[(1 - e^{-2}) - (1 - e^{-1})\big] = (1 - e^{-2})(e^{-1} - e^{-2}).$$ Numerically $0.8647 \times 0.2325 \approx 0.201$.

  3. Quadrant. Letting $x_2, y_2 \to \infty$ in (2), with $F(x, \infty) = F_X(x) = 1 - e^{-x}$ and $F(\infty, y) = F_Y(y) = 1 - e^{-2y}$: $$\P[X > 1, Y > 1] = 1 - F_X(1) - F_Y(1) + F(1, 1) = 1 - (1 - e^{-1}) - (1 - e^{-2}) + (1 - e^{-1})(1 - e^{-2}) = e^{-1}e^{-2} = e^{-3}.$$

Result

$\P[1 < X \le 2, 0 < Y \le 1] = (1 - e^{-2})(e^{-1} - e^{-2}) \approx 0.201$ and $\P[X > 1, Y > 1] = e^{-3} \approx 0.050$. Both answers factor into a part in $x$ times a part in $y$, a hint of independence that lesson 3.2 makes precise.

§ 03 DiscreteJoint PMFs

Definitionjoint PMF

If $X$ and $Y$ are discrete, their joint PMF is $P_{X,Y}(x, y) = \P[X = x, Y = y]$.

Theorem 2probabilities from the joint PMF

$P_{X,Y} \ge 0$, $\sum_{x \in S_X}\sum_{y \in S_Y} P_{X,Y}(x, y) = 1$, and for any $B \subseteq \R^2$,

$$\P[(X, Y) \in B] = \sum_{(x, y) \in B} P_{X,Y}(x, y). \tag{3}$$
Proof

The events $\{X = x, Y = y\}$ for $(x, y) \in S_X \times S_Y$ are pairwise disjoint and their union is $\Omega$; $S_X \times S_Y$ is countable. So they partition $\Omega$, and the argument of lesson 2.1, Theorem 1 applies verbatim to the random vector $(X, Y)$ in place of $X$. ∎

Examplesmaller and larger of two dice

Roll two fair dice; let $Y$ be the smaller and $X$ the larger value. For $1 \le y < x \le 6$, the outcomes $(y, x)$ and $(x, y)$ both give the pair, so $P_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{2}{36}$; for $x = y$ only $(x, x)$ does, so $P_{X,Y}(x, x) = \frac{1}{36}$; and $P_{X,Y}(x, y) = 0$ when $y > x$. The total is $6\cdot\frac1{36} + 15 \cdot\frac{2}{36} = 1$. By (3), $\P[X - Y \ge 3] = \frac{2}{36}\,\#\{(x, y) : x - y \ge 3\} = \frac{2}{36}(3 + 2 + 1) = \frac13$.

§ 04 ContinuousJoint densities

Definitionjoint PDF

$X$ and $Y$ are jointly continuous with joint PDF $f_{X,Y} \ge 0$ if

$$F_{X,Y}(x, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{x}\int_{-\infty}^{y} f_{X,Y}(u, v) \d v \d u \quad \text{for all } (x, y). \tag{4}$$
Theorem 3working with a joint density
  1. $\iint_{\R^2} f_{X,Y} = 1$, and at points where $f_{X,Y}$ is continuous, $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \dfrac{\partial^2 F_{X,Y}}{\partial x\, \partial y}(x,y)$;
  2. for every (Borel) region $B \subseteq \R^2$, $\displaystyle \P[(X, Y) \in B] = \iint_B f_{X,Y}(x, y) \d x \d y$;
  3. for $g$ with $\iint |g| f_{X,Y} < \infty$, $\displaystyle \E[g(X, Y)] = \iint g(x, y)\, f_{X,Y}(x, y) \d x \d y$.
Proof

(a) Let $x, y \to \infty$ in (4) and use Theorem 1(b). Differentiating (4) in $y$ gives $\partial_y F = \int_{-\infty}^x f(u, y) \d u$ at continuity points (fundamental theorem of calculus), and differentiating that in $x$ gives $f(x, y)$.

(b) For rectangles $B = (x_1, x_2] \times (y_1, y_2]$, combine (2) and (4): the four integrals over quadrants combine, by additivity of the integral, into $\int_{x_1}^{x_2}\int_{y_1}^{y_2} f$. Both sides of (b) are then measures on $\R^2$ (countably additive in $B$, by monotone convergence on the right) that agree on rectangles, and such measures agree on all Borel sets (the uniqueness theorem of measure theory). We take this last step from measure theory without proof.

(c) is the two-dimensional law of the unconscious statistician, which we also take as given; it reduces to (b) when $g$ is an indicator. ∎

In practice (b) is all you use: draw the region, then set up an iterated integral over it. The density is a probability per unit area: $\P[(X, Y) \in [x, x + \Delta] \times [y, y + \Delta]] \approx f_{X,Y}(x, y)\Delta^2$. Two consequences from the one-dimensional case carry over: lines and curves have probability zero (they have area zero), and densities may exceed one.

Exampleuniform on a triangle

Let $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = 2$ on $T = \{0 \le y \le x \le 1\}$ and $0$ elsewhere ($T$ has area $\tfrac12$). For $0 \le t \le 1$, the event $\{X + Y \le t\}$ is the part of $T$ below the line $x + y = t$: for each $y \in [0, t/2]$, $x$ runs from $y$ to $t - y$. So

$$\P[X + Y \le t] = \int_0^{t/2}\!\!\int_y^{t - y} 2 \d x \d y = \int_0^{t/2} 2(t - 2y) \d y = \frac{t^2}{2}.$$

For $1 \le t \le 2$ it is easier to integrate over the complement $\{X + Y > t\} \cap T$, where for each $x \in [t/2, 1]$, $y$ runs from $t - x$ to $x$: $\P[X + Y > t] = \int_{t/2}^1 2(2x - t)\d x = 2(1 - t/2)^2$. Both formulas give $\tfrac12$ at $t = 1$. This pair has a concrete origin: if $U_1, U_2$ are independent Uniform$(0, 1)$, then $X = \max(U_1, U_2)$ and $Y = \min(U_1, U_2)$ have exactly this density (exercise 4).

Worked example 3.1.2normalising a density on a triangle and integrating over a region
Problem

$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = c\,xy$ on $T = \{0 \le y \le x \le 1\}$ and $0$ elsewhere. Find $c$ and $\P[X + Y \le 1]$.

Approach

Draw the support first; the integration limits come from the picture, not from the formula. For each region, fix the outer variable, and read the range of the inner variable from the boundary lines.

Solution

  1. Normalise. On $T$, for fixed $x \in [0, 1]$, $y$ runs over $[0, x]$: $$\int_0^1\!\!\int_0^x xy \d y \d x = \int_0^1 x \cdot \frac{x^2}{2}\d x = \frac18, \qquad\text{so } c = 8.$$

  2. Describe the region. $\{X + Y \le 1\} \cap T$ is bounded by $y = 0$, $y = x$ and $x + y = 1$; the last two meet at $(\frac12, \frac12)$. With $y$ outer: $y \in [0, \frac12]$ and, for each such $y$, $x$ runs from $y$ to $1 - y$.

  3. Integrate.

    $$\P[X + Y \le 1] = \int_0^{1/2} 8y \int_y^{1-y} x \d x \d y = \int_0^{1/2} 8y\,\frac{(1-y)^2 - y^2}{2}\d y = \int_0^{1/2} 4y(1 - 2y)\d y.$$

  4. Finish. $\int_0^{1/2}(4y - 8y^2)\d y = \big[2y^2 - \tfrac83 y^3\big]_0^{1/2} = \frac12 - \frac13 = \frac16.$

Result

$c = 8$ and $\P[X + Y \le 1] = \frac16$. Choosing $y$ as the outer variable kept this to one integral; with $x$ outer the region splits at $x = \frac12$ into two pieces. Most of the work in these exercises is choosing an order of integration that avoids splits.

§ 05 In codeRectangle probabilities from a CDF

§ 06 PracticeExercises

  1. Let $G(x, y) = 1$ if $x + y \ge 0$ and $0$ otherwise. Show that $G$ satisfies Theorem 1(a)–(c) (with $G(x,\infty) = 1$) but is not a joint CDF.core

    Solution

    $G$ is non-decreasing in each argument (increasing $x$ or $y$ can only turn $x + y \ge 0$ on), right-continuous (the set $\{x + y \ge 0\}$ is closed), and tends to $0$ or $1$ as required. But the rectangle $(-1, 1] \times (-1, 1]$ gets $G(1,1) - G(-1, 1) - G(1, -1) + G(-1, -1) = 1 - 1 - 1 + 0 = -1 < 0$.

  2. For $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = c(x + y)$ on $[0, 1]^2$, find $c$ and $\P[X < Y]$, and $\P[X + Y \le 1]$.core

    Solution

    $\iint (x + y) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 = 1$, so $c = 1$. The density is symmetric in $x, y$ and the diagonal has probability zero, so $\P[X < Y] = \tfrac12$. And $\P[X + Y \le 1] = \int_0^1\int_0^{1-x}(x + y)\d y\d x = \int_0^1 \tfrac{1 - x^2}{2}\d x = \tfrac13$.

  3. In the dice example, compute $\E[X - Y]$ from the joint PMF.core

    Solution

    The diagonal contributes $0$. For a gap $d = x - y \in \{1, \dots, 5\}$ there are $6 - d$ pairs, each of probability $\tfrac2{36}$: $\E[X - Y] = \frac{2}{36}\sum_{d=1}^5 d(6 - d) = \frac{2}{36}(5 + 8 + 9 + 8 + 5) = \frac{70}{36} \approx 1.94$.

  4. Let $U_1, U_2$ be independent Uniform$(0,1)$, $X = \max(U_1, U_2)$, $Y = \min(U_1, U_2)$. Show $F_{X,Y}(x, y) = 2xy - y^2$ for $0 \le y \le x \le 1$ and $x^2$ for $0 \le x \le y \le 1$, and deduce the joint density.stretch

    Solution

    For $y \le x$: $\{X \le x\} = \{U_1 \le x, U_2 \le x\}$, probability $x^2$. Within it, $\{Y > y\}$ means both $U_i \in (y, x]$, probability $(x - y)^2$ (independence lets the probabilities multiply). So $F = x^2 - (x - y)^2 = 2xy - y^2$. For $x \le y$: $X \le x$ forces $Y \le X \le x \le y$, so $F = \P[X \le x] = x^2$. Then $\partial^2 F/\partial x \partial y = \partial_x(2x - 2y) = 2$ on $y < x$ and $0$ on $y > x$.
