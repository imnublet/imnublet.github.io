Conditional distributions and conditional expectation

Observe $Y$, and $X$ gets a new distribution. Its mean is the best possible prediction of $X$ from $Y$.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 30 min Y&G ch. 7
After this lesson you can
  • condition a random variable on an event, and on the value of another random variable;
  • justify the conditional density $f_{X|Y} = f_{X,Y}/f_Y$ as a limit, and know where that limit is fragile;
  • treat $\E[X \mid Y]$ as a random variable and prove the tower property and the law of total variance;
  • prove that $\E[X \mid Y]$ is the best predictor of $X$ from $Y$ in mean squared error.

§ 01 EventsConditioning on an event

Lesson 1.3 conditioned events on events. Conditioning a random variable on an event $B$ with $\P[B] > 0$ means using the probability measure $\P[\,\cdot \mid B]$ in place of $\P$ throughout. So $X$ given $B$ has a conditional PMF $P_{X|B}(x) = \P[X = x \mid B]$, a conditional CDF $F_{X|B}(x) = \P[X \le x \mid B]$, and when $B = \{X \in A\}$ for continuous $X$, a conditional density

$$f_{X|B}(x) = \frac{f_X(x)}{\P[X \in A]} \quad (x \in A), \qquad 0 \text{ otherwise}. \tag{1}$$

(Differentiate $F_{X|B}(x) = \P[X \le x, X \in A]/\P[B] = \int_{(-\infty, x] \cap A} f_X / \P[B]$.) The shape inside $A$ is kept and renormalised; outside $A$ it is erased. Conditional expectations follow: $\E[X \mid B] = \int x\,f_{X|B}(x)\d x$. Example: memorylessness says that for $X \sim \text{Exp}(\lambda)$, $X - s$ given $\{X > s\}$ is again Exp$(\lambda)$, so $\E[X \mid X > s] = s + 1/\lambda$.

§ 02 VariablesConditioning on $Y = y$

Definitionconditional PMF and density

For discrete $X, Y$ and $P_Y(y) > 0$: $\displaystyle P_{X|Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{P_{X,Y}(x, y)}{P_Y(y)}$.

For jointly continuous $X, Y$ and $f_Y(y) > 0$:

$$f_{X|Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{f_{X,Y}(x, y)}{f_Y(y)}. \tag{2}$$

The discrete definition is just $\P[X = x \mid Y = y]$. The continuous one cannot be, since $\P[Y = y] = 0$. It is justified as a limit of conditioning on thin slabs.

Theorem 1the conditional density as a limit

Suppose $f_{X,Y}$ is continuous and bounded near the line $\{(u, y) : u \in \R\}$, dominated there by an integrable function of $u$, and $f_Y(y) > 0$. Then for every $x$,

$$\lim_{\Delta \to 0}\P[X \le x \mid y < Y \le y + \Delta] = \int_{-\infty}^{x} f_{X|Y}(u \mid y)\d u.$$
Proof

By definition of conditional probability and lesson 3.1, Theorem 3(b),

$$\P[X \le x \mid y < Y \le y + \Delta] = \frac{\int_{-\infty}^x \frac1\Delta\int_y^{y+\Delta} f_{X,Y}(u, v)\d v \d u}{\frac1\Delta\int_y^{y + \Delta} f_Y(v)\d v}.$$

The inner average $\frac1\Delta\int_y^{y+\Delta} f_{X,Y}(u, v)\d v \to f_{X,Y}(u, y)$ for each $u$ by continuity (as in the sifting proof of lesson 2.5), and dominated convergence passes the limit through $\int_{-\infty}^x \d u$. The denominator tends to $f_Y(y) > 0$ by the same argument applied to $f_Y = \int f_{X,Y}\d u$. The ratio of limits is the right side. ∎

The Borel paradox

Conditioning on a probability-zero event depends on how the event is approached. A point uniform on a sphere, conditioned to lie on a great circle, is uniform along it if the circle is the equator approached by latitude bands, but not if it is a meridian approached by longitude wedges (the wedges are fatter near the equator). Formula (2) is the limit along slabs $\{y < Y \le y + \Delta\}$ in the variable $Y$; reparameterise $Y$ and the conditional density given "the same" event can change. In practice, condition on the random variable you actually observe.

For each fixed $y$, (2) is a density in $x$: non-negative, and integrating to $\int f_{X,Y}(x, y)\d x / f_Y(y) = 1$ by lesson 3.2. Rearranged, $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_{X|Y}(x \mid y)\,f_Y(y)$: the joint law can be built by drawing $Y$ first and then $X$ from its conditional law, which is how generative models are specified. And Bayes' rule holds for densities,

$$f_{Y|X}(y \mid x) = \frac{f_{X|Y}(x \mid y)\,f_Y(y)}{\int f_{X|Y}(x \mid y')\,f_Y(y')\d y'}, \tag{3}$$

by writing $f_{X,Y}$ both ways and using (2) of lesson 3.2 for the denominator. $X$ and $Y$ are independent exactly when $f_{X|Y}(x \mid y) = f_X(x)$ wherever $f_Y(y) > 0$.

Examplethe triangle again

For $f_{X,Y} = 2$ on $\{0 \le y \le x \le 1\}$, $f_Y(y) = 2(1 - y)$, so for $0 \le y < 1$, $f_{X|Y}(x \mid y) = \frac{2}{2(1-y)} = \frac{1}{1 - y}$ on $y \le x \le 1$: given the smaller of two uniforms, the larger is uniform on the rest of the interval. Similarly $f_{Y|X}(y \mid x) = 1/x$ on $[0, x]$.

§ 03 MeansConditional expectation as a random variable

Definitionconditional expectation

$\E[X \mid Y = y] = \sum_x x\,P_{X|Y}(x \mid y)$ or $\int x\,f_{X|Y}(x \mid y)\d x$, a number for each $y$. Writing it as a function $h(y)$, the conditional expectation of $X$ given $Y$ is the random variable

$$\E[X \mid Y] = h(Y). \tag{4}$$

In the triangle example, $\E[X \mid Y = y] = \frac{1 + y}{2}$ (the midpoint of $[y, 1]$), so $\E[X \mid Y] = \frac{1 + Y}{2}$, and $\E[Y \mid X] = X/2$. $\E[X \mid Y]$ is "$X$ averaged over everything except the information in $Y$". Conditional versions of every property of expectation hold, since $f_{X|Y}(\cdot \mid y)$ is a density: linearity, $\E[g(Y)X \mid Y] = g(Y)\E[X \mid Y]$ ("taking out what is known", because given $Y = y$ the factor $g(y)$ is a constant), and $\E[X \mid Y] = \E[X]$ when $X, Y$ are independent.

Theorem 2tower property (law of iterated expectations)

If $\E|X| < \infty$, then $\E\big[\E[X \mid Y]\big] = \E[X]$. More generally $\E\big[g(Y)\,\E[X \mid Y]\big] = \E[g(Y)\,X]$ whenever $\E|g(Y)X| < \infty$.

Proof

Jointly continuous case. By LOTUS for the function $h(Y)g(Y)$, then (2), then Fubini (justified by the integrability assumption, after running the same computation with absolute values):

$$\E[g(Y)h(Y)] = \int g(y)\Big(\int x\,\frac{f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_Y(y)}\d x\Big) f_Y(y)\d y = \iint g(y)\,x\,f_{X,Y}(x, y)\d x\d y = \E[g(Y)X].$$

Take $g = 1$ for the first statement. The discrete case is identical with sums. ∎

The tower property is the law of total probability for means: average the conditional means with the weights of $Y$. In the triangle, $\E[X] = \E[(1 + Y)/2] = (1 + \tfrac13)/2 = \tfrac23$, as computed directly in lesson 3.3.

Theorem 3law of total variance

If $\E[X^2] < \infty$, then with $\Var[X \mid Y] = \E\big[(X - \E[X \mid Y])^2 \mid Y\big]$,

$$\Var[X] = \E\big[\Var[X \mid Y]\big] + \Var\big[\E[X \mid Y]\big]. \tag{5}$$
Proof

Write $h = \E[X \mid Y]$ and $X - \mu_X = (X - h) + (h - \mu_X)$. Squaring and taking expectations,

$$\Var[X] = \E[(X - h)^2] + 2\,\E[(X - h)(h - \mu_X)] + \E[(h - \mu_X)^2].$$

The first term is $\E\big[\E[(X - h)^2 \mid Y]\big] = \E[\Var[X \mid Y]]$ by the tower property. The last is $\Var[h]$, since $\E[h] = \mu_X$ by the tower property. The middle term vanishes: $h - \mu_X = g(Y)$ is a function of $Y$, and Theorem 2 gives $\E[g(Y)X] = \E[g(Y)h]$, i.e. $\E[(X - h)g(Y)] = 0$. ∎

Total variance = unexplained (within-group) + explained (between-group) variance: the decomposition behind ANOVA, behind the $R^2$ of a regression, and behind the split of predictive uncertainty into aleatoric and epistemic parts in Bayesian deep learning.

Worked example 3.5.1a conditional mean, and the tower property as a check
Problem

$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = x + y$ on $[0, 1]^2$. Find $f_{X|Y}$, $\E[X \mid Y = y]$ and the random variable $\E[X \mid Y]$. Verify the tower property.

Approach

Marginal of the conditioning variable first, then divide (2), then integrate against $x$. The tower property is a free consistency check: averaging the conditional mean over $f_Y$ must give $\E[X]$ computed directly.

Solution

  1. Marginal. $f_Y(y) = \int_0^1 (x + y)\d x = \frac12 + y$ for $0 \le y \le 1$.

  2. Conditional density. $f_{X|Y}(x \mid y) = \dfrac{x + y}{y + \frac12}$ for $0 \le x \le 1$: for each $y$, a linear density in $x$.

  3. Conditional mean.

    $$\E[X \mid Y = y] = \frac{\int_0^1 x(x + y)\d x}{y + \frac12} = \frac{\frac13 + \frac y2}{y + \frac12} = \frac{2 + 3y}{3(1 + 2y)},$$ so $\E[X \mid Y] = \dfrac{2 + 3Y}{3(1 + 2Y)}$, which decreases from $\frac23$ at $Y = 0$ to $\frac59$ at $Y = 1$.

  4. Tower check. $\E\big[\E[X \mid Y]\big] = \int_0^1 \frac{\frac13 + \frac y2}{y + \frac12}\big(y + \tfrac12\big)\d y = \int_0^1 \big(\tfrac13 + \tfrac y2\big)\d y = \tfrac13 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac7{12}$. By symmetry $f_X(x) = x + \frac12$, and directly $\E[X] = \int_0^1 x(x + \tfrac12)\d x = \tfrac7{12}$ ✓.

Result

$\E[X \mid Y = y] = \frac{2 + 3y}{3(1 + 2y)}$. It is not linear in $y$, so the best predictor of lesson 3.3 (a straight line) is strictly worse here, by Theorem 4. Note how $f_Y$ cancelled in the tower check: that cancellation is the proof of Theorem 2 in miniature.

Worked example 3.5.2a hierarchical model and the law of total variance
Problem

The click-through rate $P$ of an ad is unknown and modelled as $\text{Uniform}(0, 1)$. Given $P = p$, the ad is shown $n$ times and the number of clicks is $K \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$. Find $\E[K]$ and $\Var[K]$, and compare with a Binomial$(n, \frac12)$ count.

Approach

Two-stage models are what the tower property and the law of total variance are for: compute the conditional mean and variance given the first stage, which are known binomial formulas, then average over the first stage.

Solution

  1. Given the first stage. $\E[K \mid P] = nP$ and $\Var[K \mid P] = nP(1 - P)$ (Theorem 4 of lesson 2.2).

  2. Mean. By Theorem 2, $\E[K] = \E[nP] = \frac n2$.

  3. Total variance. By (5), with $\E[P] = \frac12$, $\E[P^2] = \frac13$, $\Var[P] = \frac1{12}$: $$\Var[K] = \underbrace{\E[nP(1 - P)]}_{n(\frac12 - \frac13) = \frac n6} + \underbrace{\Var[nP]}_{\frac{n^2}{12}} = \frac{n(n + 2)}{12}.$$

  4. Compare. A Binomial$(n, \frac12)$ count has the same mean but variance $\frac n4$. For $n = 10$: $\Var[K] = 10$ against $2.5$.

Result

$\E[K] = \frac n2$ and $\Var[K] = \frac{n(n+2)}{12}$, which grows like $n^2$ rather than $n$: uncertainty about $P$ does not average out with more impressions. (In fact $K$ is uniform on $\{0, \dots, n\}$, whose variance is the same $\frac{(n+1)^2 - 1}{12}$.) This "overdispersion" is why a plain binomial model underestimates the spread of real click counts.

§ 04 PredictionThe conditional mean is the best predictor

Theorem 4MMSE property

If $\E[X^2] < \infty$, then for every function $g$ with $\E[g(Y)^2] < \infty$,

$$\E\big[(X - g(Y))^2\big] = \E\big[(X - \E[X \mid Y])^2\big] + \E\big[(\E[X \mid Y] - g(Y))^2\big] \ge \E\big[(X - \E[X \mid Y])^2\big]. \tag{6}$$
Proof

With $h(Y) = \E[X \mid Y]$, expand $X - g = (X - h) + (h - g)$. The cross term is $2\,\E[(X - h(Y))\,(h(Y) - g(Y))]$, which is $0$ by the orthogonality shown at the end of the previous proof, applied to the function $h - g$ of $Y$. ∎

So among all functions of $Y$, linear or not, $\E[X \mid Y]$ minimises the mean squared error, and the error of any other predictor exceeds the minimum by exactly its mean squared distance from $\E[X \mid Y]$. Geometrically, $\E[X \mid Y]$ is the orthogonal projection of $X$ onto the space of square-integrable functions of $Y$; the best linear predictor of lesson 3.3 is the projection onto the smaller space of affine functions. A regression network trained with squared loss is estimating $\E[X \mid Y]$, which is why its outputs are conditional means, not typical values.

§ 06 PracticeExercises

  1. In the triangle example, verify (5) for $X$ given $Y$.core

    Solution

    Given $Y = y$, $X$ is uniform on $[y, 1]$, so $\Var[X \mid Y] = (1 - Y)^2/12$. With $f_Y(y) = 2(1-y)$, $\E[(1 - Y)^2] = \int_0^1 2(1-y)^3\d y = \tfrac12$, so $\E[\Var[X \mid Y]] = \tfrac1{24}$. And $\Var[(1 + Y)/2] = \tfrac14\Var[Y] = \tfrac14 \cdot \tfrac1{18} = \tfrac1{72}$. The sum is $\tfrac{3}{72} + \tfrac{1}{72} = \tfrac1{18} = \Var[X]$, as in lesson 3.3.

  2. (Poisson thinning.) $N \sim \text{Poisson}(\alpha)$ and, given $N = n$, $K \sim \text{Binomial}(n, p)$. Show $K \sim \text{Poisson}(\alpha p)$.core

    Solution

    By total probability over $n \ge k$, $$P_K(k) = \sum_{n \ge k}\frac{\alpha^n e^{-\alpha}}{n!}\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k} = \frac{(\alpha p)^k e^{-\alpha}}{k!}\sum_{m \ge 0}\frac{(\alpha(1-p))^m}{m!} = \frac{(\alpha p)^k e^{-\alpha p}}{k!},$$ with $m = n - k$. Randomly keeping each event of a Poisson stream with probability $p$ leaves a Poisson stream.

  3. (Random sums.) $N$ is a non-negative integer variable, $X_1, X_2, \dots$ are iid with mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$, independent of $N$, and $S = \sum_{i=1}^N X_i$. Show $\E[S] = \E[N]\mu$ and $\Var[S] = \E[N]\sigma^2 + \Var[N]\mu^2$.core

    Solution

    Given $N = n$, $S$ is a sum of $n$ independent terms, so $\E[S \mid N] = N\mu$ and $\Var[S \mid N] = N\sigma^2$. The tower property gives $\E[S] = \mu\E[N]$, and (5) gives $\Var[S] = \E[N\sigma^2] + \Var[N\mu]$. With $N \sim$ Poisson$(4)$ and Exp$(1)$ terms: $4$ and $4 + 4 = 8$.

  4. $\Lambda \sim \text{Exp}(1)$ and, given $\Lambda = \lambda$, $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda)$. Find $P_X$, $\E[X]$ and $\Var[X]$.core

    Solution

    $P_X(k) = \int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k e^{-\lambda}}{k!}\d\lambda = \frac{1}{k!}\int_0^\infty \lambda^k e^{-2\lambda}\d\lambda = \frac{1}{k!}\cdot\frac{k!}{2^{k+1}} = 2^{-(k+1)}$: geometric (failures before the first success, $p = \tfrac12$). $\E[X] = \E[\Lambda] = 1$, and by (5) $\Var[X] = \E[\Lambda] + \Var[\Lambda] = 2$: mixing over the rate inflates the variance above the Poisson value. This overdispersion is why count data are often modelled as negative binomial.

  5. Show that if $\E[X \mid Y] = a + bY$ is affine, then it coincides with the best linear predictor of lesson 3.3.stretch

    Solution

    By (6), $\E[X \mid Y]$ minimises the MSE over all functions of $Y$, hence over affine ones; the best linear predictor is the unique minimiser over affine functions (the quadratic in lesson 3.3 is strictly convex when $\Var[Y] > 0$). So they are equal, and $b = \Cov[X,Y]/\Var[Y]$. Lesson 3.6 shows this happens for every bivariate Gaussian.
