Bivariate Gaussian random variables

Five parameters, elliptical contours, and conditionals that are Gaussian with a linear mean.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 30 min Y&G ch. 5, 7
After this lesson you can
  • write the bivariate Gaussian density and interpret its five parameters;
  • build a bivariate Gaussian from two independent standard normals, and factor its density into a marginal and a conditional;
  • state and prove that its marginals are Gaussian and its conditionals Gaussian with a linear mean;
  • prove that jointly Gaussian and uncorrelated implies independent, and that linear combinations are Gaussian;
  • compute the probability inside a contour ellipse.

§ 01 DefinitionThe bivariate normal density

Definitionbivariate Gaussian

$X$ and $Y$ are bivariate Gaussian with parameters $\mu_1, \mu_2 \in \R$, $\sigma_1, \sigma_2 > 0$ and $\rho \in (-1, 1)$ if

$$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma_1\sigma_2\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}}\exp\!\Big(-\frac{q(x, y)}{2}\Big), \qquad q(x, y) = \frac{z_1^2 - 2\rho z_1 z_2 + z_2^2}{1 - \rho^2}, \tag{1}$$

where $z_1 = (x - \mu_1)/\sigma_1$ and $z_2 = (y - \mu_2)/\sigma_2$ are the standardised coordinates.

The quadratic form $q$ is positive definite when $|\rho| < 1$ (its discriminant $4\rho^2 - 4 < 0$), so the density decays in every direction and its level sets $\{q = c\}$ are ellipses. In lesson 4.4 the same formula is written as $\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det \Sigma}}\exp\big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)^{\mathsf T}\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x - \boldsymbol\mu)\big)$ with a $2 \times 2$ covariance matrix $\Sigma$; here we work with coordinates, which keeps every step elementary.

§ 02 StructureOne square completed

Everything about the bivariate Gaussian follows from one algebraic identity. Completing the square in $z_2$,

$$z_1^2 - 2\rho z_1z_2 + z_2^2 = (1 - \rho^2)\,z_1^2 + (z_2 - \rho z_1)^2,$$

as expanding the right side confirms. Dividing by $1 - \rho^2$,

$$q(x, y) = z_1^2 + \frac{(z_2 - \rho z_1)^2}{1 - \rho^2}. \tag{2}$$
Theorem 1factorisation into marginal times conditional

The density (1) factors as $f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_X(x)\,f_{Y|X}(y \mid x)$ with

$$X \sim \mathcal N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2), \qquad Y \mid X = x \;\sim\; \mathcal N\Big(\mu_2 + \rho\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}(x - \mu_1),\;\; \sigma_2^2(1 - \rho^2)\Big). \tag{3}$$

By symmetry $Y \sim \mathcal N(\mu_2, \sigma_2^2)$, and $X \mid Y = y$ is Gaussian with mean $\mu_1 + \rho\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_2}(y - \mu_2)$ and variance $\sigma_1^2(1 - \rho^2)$.

Proof

Insert (2) into (1) and split the exponential and the constant:

$$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_1}e^{-z_1^2/2}}_{g(x)}\;\cdot\;\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_2\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{(z_2 - \rho z_1)^2}{2(1 - \rho^2)}\Big)}_{k(y; x)}.$$

Since $\sigma_2(z_2 - \rho z_1) = y - \mu_2 - \rho\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}(x - \mu_1) = y - m(x)$, the second factor is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi s^2}}e^{-(y - m(x))^2/2s^2}$ with $s^2 = \sigma_2^2(1-\rho^2)$: for each fixed $x$, the $\mathcal N(m(x), s^2)$ density in $y$, which integrates to $1$. So by lesson 3.2, Theorem 1, $f_X(x) = \int f_{X,Y}(x, y)\d y = g(x)$, the $\mathcal N(\mu_1, \sigma_1^2)$ density, and then $f_{Y|X}(y \mid x) = f_{X,Y}/f_X = k(y; x)$ by definition (lesson 3.5). In particular (1) integrates to $1$. The statements for $Y$ follow by exchanging the roles of $x$ and $y$, as $q$ is symmetric under $(z_1, z_2) \leftrightarrow (z_2, z_1)$. ∎

Three facts are packed into (3).

  • The conditional mean is linear in $x$, so by lesson 3.5, exercise 5, $\E[Y \mid X]$ is the best linear predictor of lesson 3.3. For Gaussians, linear regression loses nothing.
  • The conditional variance does not depend on $x$ and is smaller than $\sigma_2^2$ by the factor $1 - \rho^2$: observing $X$ explains the fraction $\rho^2$ of the variance of $Y$, whatever value is observed (homoscedasticity).
  • Regression to the mean: in standard units the conditional mean is $\rho z_1$, and $|\rho| < 1$ pulls predictions towards the mean. Children of very tall parents are tall, but on average less extreme.
Theorem 2ρ is the correlation coefficient

$\Cov[X, Y] = \rho\,\sigma_1\sigma_2$, so $\rho_{X,Y} = \rho$.

Proof

By the tower property and taking out what is known (lesson 3.5), $\E[(X - \mu_1)(Y - \mu_2)] = \E\big[(X - \mu_1)\,\E[Y - \mu_2 \mid X]\big] = \E\big[(X - \mu_1)\cdot\rho\tfrac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}(X - \mu_1)\big] = \rho\tfrac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}\sigma_1^2$. ∎

Worked example 3.6.1predicting a final exam from a midterm
Problem

Midterm and final scores $(X, Y)$ are bivariate Gaussian with $\mu_1 = 60$, $\sigma_1 = 10$, $\mu_2 = 65$, $\sigma_2 = 12$, $\rho = 0.6$. A student scored $75$ on the midterm. Find the distribution of their final score, and the probability it exceeds $80$. Compare with a student about whom nothing is known.

Approach

Theorem 1 gives the conditional law of $Y$ given $X = x$ in closed form: a Gaussian with a mean on the regression line and a reduced variance. Plug in, then standardise as in lesson 2.4.

Solution

  1. Conditional mean. $\mu_2 + \rho\frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1}(x - \mu_1) = 65 + 0.6 \cdot \frac{12}{10}\cdot 15 = 65 + 10.8 = 75.8$.

  2. Conditional variance. $\sigma_2^2(1 - \rho^2) = 144 \times 0.64 = 92.16$, standard deviation $9.6$. So $Y \mid X = 75 \sim \mathcal N(75.8,\ 9.6^2)$.

  3. Probability. $\P[Y > 80 \mid X = 75] = Q\big(\frac{80 - 75.8}{9.6}\big) = Q(0.4375) \approx 0.331$.

  4. Without the midterm. $Y \sim \mathcal N(65, 12^2)$, so $\P[Y > 80] = Q(1.25) \approx 0.106$.

Result

$Y \mid X = 75 \sim \mathcal N(75.8, 9.6^2)$, and the chance of beating $80$ rises from $0.11$ to $0.33$. The midterm is $1.5$ standard deviations above its mean, but the prediction is only $0.6 \times 1.5 = 0.9$ standard deviations above the final's mean: regression to the mean, with the factor $\rho$.

§ 03 ConstructionFrom two independent normals

Theorem 3construction

Let $Z_1, Z_2 \iid \mathcal N(0, 1)$. Then

$$X = \mu_1 + \sigma_1 Z_1, \qquad Y = \mu_2 + \sigma_2\big(\rho Z_1 + \sqrt{1 - \rho^2}\,Z_2\big) \tag{4}$$

are bivariate Gaussian with density (1).

Proof

The map $(z_1, z_2) \mapsto (x, y)$ in (4) is an affine bijection of $\R^2$; its inverse is $z_1 = (x - \mu_1)/\sigma_1$, $z_2' = \big((y - \mu_2)/\sigma_2 - \rho z_1\big)/\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}$, a triangular linear map with Jacobian determinant $\frac{1}{\sigma_1}\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma_2\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}}$. By the change-of-variables formula (lesson 3.4, Theorem 4) and independence,

$$f_{X,Y}(x, y) = \frac{1}{2\pi}e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2'^2)/2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sigma_1\sigma_2\sqrt{1 - \rho^2}},$$

and by (2), $z_1^2 + z_2'^2 = q(x, y)$. ∎

Equation (4) is how bivariate Gaussians are sampled (the demo does exactly this), and it is the $2 \times 2$ case of $\mathbf X = \boldsymbol\mu + L\mathbf Z$ with $L$ the Cholesky factor of $\Sigma$ (lesson 4.4). It also explains why $\rho$ must lie strictly inside $(-1, 1)$ for a density to exist: at $|\rho| = 1$, $Y$ is an affine function of $X$ and the pair lives on a line.

§ 04 ConsequencesIndependence, linear combinations, ellipses

Theorem 4uncorrelated jointly Gaussian variables are independent

If $X, Y$ are bivariate Gaussian with $\rho = 0$, they are independent.

Proof

With $\rho = 0$, $q = z_1^2 + z_2^2$ and (1) is $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_1}e^{-z_1^2/2}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma_2}e^{-z_2^2/2} = f_X(x)f_Y(y)$ on all of $\R^2$; apply lesson 3.2, Theorem 2(c). ∎

The hypothesis is joint Gaussianity. Two Gaussian marginals with zero correlation are not enough: the random-sign pair $Y = SX$ of lessons 3.2–3.3 has $\mathcal N(0,1)$ marginals and $\rho = 0$, yet $|Y| = |X|$. It is not bivariate Gaussian (its joint law sits on the two diagonals).

Theorem 5linear combinations are Gaussian

If $X, Y$ are bivariate Gaussian, then for constants $a, b$ not both zero,

$$aX + bY \sim \mathcal N\big(a\mu_1 + b\mu_2,\;\; a^2\sigma_1^2 + 2ab\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 + b^2\sigma_2^2\big). \tag{5}$$
Proof

By Theorem 3 we may take $(X, Y)$ as in (4), so $aX + bY = c + \alpha Z_1 + \beta Z_2$ for constants $c, \alpha, \beta$. It suffices to show $\alpha Z_1 + \beta Z_2 \sim \mathcal N(0, \alpha^2 + \beta^2)$ when $(\alpha, \beta) \ne 0$. Let $r = \sqrt{\alpha^2 + \beta^2}$ and rotate: $W_1 = (\alpha Z_1 + \beta Z_2)/r$, $W_2 = (-\beta Z_1 + \alpha Z_2)/r$. This is an orthogonal linear map, with $|\det| = 1$ and $w_1^2 + w_2^2 = z_1^2 + z_2^2$. Since the density of $(Z_1, Z_2)$, $\frac1{2\pi}e^{-(z_1^2 + z_2^2)/2}$, depends only on $z_1^2 + z_2^2$, the change-of-variables formula gives $(W_1, W_2)$ the same density. So $W_1 \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$ and $\alpha Z_1 + \beta Z_2 = rW_1 \sim \mathcal N(0, r^2)$. The mean and variance in (5) then follow from linearity and Theorem 2 of lesson 3.3 with $\Cov[X, Y] = \rho\sigma_1\sigma_2$. ∎

Rotation invariance of the standard bivariate normal is the deep fact here: it is the only distribution with independent coordinates that looks the same in every orthonormal frame (Maxwell's characterisation). It is why projections of Gaussian data onto any direction are Gaussian, the basis of PCA's probabilistic interpretation and of random projections.

Theorem 6probability inside a contour

$q(X, Y)$ has the Exp$(\tfrac12)$ law (chi-squared with two degrees of freedom), so the ellipse $\{q \le c\}$ has probability

$$\P\big[q(X, Y) \le c\big] = 1 - e^{-c/2}. \tag{6}$$
Proof

In the construction (4), $z_1 = Z_1$ and $z_2' = Z_2$, so by (2), $q(X, Y) = Z_1^2 + Z_2^2 = R^2$ in the polar notation of lesson 3.4, Theorem 5, which showed $R^2 \sim \text{Exp}(\tfrac12)$. ∎

The "one-sigma ellipse" $q \le 1$ therefore holds only $1 - e^{-1/2} \approx 39.3\%$ of the mass, not the $68.3\%$ of the one-dimensional $\pm1\sigma$ interval; the $95\%$ ellipse is $q \le -2\ln 0.05 \approx 5.99$. Confidence ellipses drawn with the one-dimensional numbers are too small.

Worked example 3.6.2does the student improve? and a 90% ellipse
Problem

With the scores of worked example 3.6.1, find (a) $\P[Y > X]$, the probability of a better final than midterm, and (b) the constant $c$ for which the ellipse $\{q(x, y) \le c\}$ contains $90\%$ of students.

Approach

(a) is a question about one linear combination, $D = Y - X$, so use Theorem 5 to get its law and then standardise. (b) is an inversion of (6).

Solution

  1. Law of the difference. With $a = -1$, $b = 1$ in (5): mean $65 - 60 = 5$, variance $$\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_2^2 - 2\rho\sigma_1\sigma_2 = 100 + 144 - 2 \cdot 0.6 \cdot 10 \cdot 12 = 100,$$ so $D \sim \mathcal N(5, 10^2)$.

  2. (a) Probability. $\P[Y > X] = \P[D > 0] = \P\big[\tfrac{D - 5}{10} > -0.5\big] = \Phi(0.5) \approx 0.691$.

  3. (b) Invert (6). $1 - e^{-c/2} = 0.9 \iff e^{-c/2} = 0.1 \iff c = 2\ln 10 \approx 4.61$.

Result

(a) $\approx 0.69$. Note how the positive correlation shrinks the variance of the difference (to $100$, from $244$ if independent): correlated scores move together. (b) $c = 2\ln 10$; in the standardised coordinates the $90\%$ region is the ellipse $z_1^2 - 1.2z_1z_2 + z_2^2 \le 0.64 \times 4.61 \approx 2.95$.

§ 06 PracticeExercises

  1. For a standard bivariate Gaussian ($\mu = 0$, $\sigma_1 = \sigma_2 = 1$), find the law of $X + Y$ and of $X - Y$, and show they are independent.core

    Solution

    By (5), $X + Y \sim \mathcal N(0, 2 + 2\rho)$ and $X - Y \sim \mathcal N(0, 2 - 2\rho)$. For independence, find the joint density of $(U, V) = (X + Y, X - Y)$. The inverse map is $x = (u + v)/2$, $y = (u - v)/2$ with $|\det J| = \tfrac12$. Since $x^2 + y^2 = (u^2 + v^2)/2$ and $xy = (u^2 - v^2)/4$, $$q = \frac{(u^2 + v^2)/2 - \rho(u^2 - v^2)/2}{1 - \rho^2} = \frac{u^2}{2(1 + \rho)} + \frac{v^2}{2(1 - \rho)}.$$ So $f_{U,V}$ is a constant times $e^{-u^2/4(1+\rho)}e^{-v^2/4(1-\rho)}$ on all of $\R^2$, a product, and $U, V$ are independent by lesson 3.2, Theorem 3.

  2. Heights of fathers and sons (in cm) are bivariate Gaussian with means $175$ and $177$, standard deviations $7$ and $7$, $\rho = 0.5$. Predict a son's height when his father is $189$ cm, and give a $95\%$ prediction interval.core

    Solution

    By (3), the conditional mean is $177 + 0.5 \cdot \frac77(189 - 175) = 184$ and the conditional standard deviation $7\sqrt{0.75} \approx 6.06$. The interval is $184 \pm 1.96 \cdot 6.06 \approx [172.1, 195.9]$. The father is $2\sigma$ above average; the predicted son only $1\sigma$.

  3. Show that $\Var[Y \mid X] \le \Var[Y]$ for a bivariate Gaussian, with equality iff $X, Y$ are independent.core

    Solution

    $\Var[Y \mid X] = \sigma_2^2(1 - \rho^2) \le \sigma_2^2$, with equality iff $\rho = 0$, which by Theorem 4 (and the converse, lesson 3.3) is independence. For general laws the conditional variance can exceed $\Var[Y]$ for some values of $X$; only its average cannot, by the law of total variance.

  4. (Sheppard's formula.) For a standard bivariate Gaussian, show $\P[X > 0, Y > 0] = \frac14 + \frac{\arcsin\rho}{2\pi}$.stretch

    Solution

    Write $\rho = \cos\alpha$ with $\alpha \in (0, \pi)$, so $\sqrt{1 - \rho^2} = \sin\alpha$, and in polar form $Z_1 = R\cos\Theta$, $Z_2 = R\sin\Theta$ with $\Theta$ uniform and independent of $R$ (lesson 3.4). By (4), $X = R\cos\Theta$ and $Y = R(\cos\alpha\cos\Theta + \sin\alpha\sin\Theta) = R\cos(\Theta - \alpha)$. Since $R > 0$, the event is $\{\cos\Theta > 0, \cos(\Theta - \alpha) > 0\}$: $\Theta$ within $\pi/2$ of both $0$ and $\alpha$, an arc of length $\pi - \alpha$. So the probability is $\frac{\pi - \alpha}{2\pi} = \frac12 - \frac{\arccos\rho}{2\pi} = \frac14 + \frac{\arcsin\rho}{2\pi}$, using $\arccos\rho = \frac\pi2 - \arcsin\rho$.
