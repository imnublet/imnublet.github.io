Expectation, variance and moments

The expectation compresses a distribution to one number, and the variance says how much that compression loses.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 25 min Y&G ch. 3
After this lesson you can
  • define $\E[X]$ for a discrete random variable, including when it fails to exist;
  • compute $\E[g(X)]$ directly from $P_X$ (the law of the unconscious statistician);
  • use the tail-sum formula for non-negative integer variables;
  • derive the mean and variance of the Bernoulli, binomial, geometric and Poisson laws;
  • prove the Markov and Chebyshev inequalities and the mean-squared-error decomposition.

§ 01 DefinitionThe expected value

A PMF is a whole function. Often we want one number that says where the distribution sits. The natural choice weights each value by its probability, like a centre of mass with masses $P_X(x)$ at positions $x$.

Definitionexpected value

Let $X$ be discrete. If $\sum_{x \in S_X} |x|\,P_X(x) < \infty$, the expected value (mean, expectation) of $X$ is

$$\E[X] = \mu_X = \sum_{x \in S_X} x\, P_X(x). \tag{1}$$

Otherwise $\E[X]$ is undefined. If $X \ge 0$ the sum always makes sense in $[0, \infty]$, and we write $\E[X] = \infty$ when it diverges.

Why demand absolute convergence? Because $S_X$ has no preferred order, and a series that converges only conditionally can be rearranged to any value (Riemann's rearrangement theorem). Absolute convergence makes the sum independent of the order, so $\E[X]$ is well defined. The connection to averages is the law of large numbers (lesson 5.1): the average of many independent copies of $X$ converges to $\E[X]$, and when $\E[X] = \infty$ the average drifts off to infinity instead, as the demo below shows.

§ 02 ToolsTwo ways to compute an expectation

Theorem 1law of the unconscious statistician

Let $X$ be discrete, $g : \R \to \R$ and $Y = g(X)$. If $\sum_{x} |g(x)|\, P_X(x) < \infty$, then $\E[Y]$ exists and

$$\E[g(X)] = \sum_{x \in S_X} g(x)\, P_X(x). \tag{2}$$
Proof

The definition says $\E[Y] = \sum_{y \in S_Y} y\, P_Y(y)$. By the PMF of a function (lesson 2.1, Theorem 4), $P_Y(y) = \sum_{x : g(x) = y} P_X(x)$, so

$$\sum_{y \in S_Y} |y|\, P_Y(y) = \sum_{y \in S_Y} \sum_{x : g(x) = y} |g(x)|\, P_X(x) = \sum_{x \in S_X} |g(x)|\, P_X(x) < \infty,$$

where the middle step regroups a series of non-negative terms: the sets $\{x : g(x) = y\}$ partition $S_X$. So $\E[Y]$ exists. Repeating the same regrouping without absolute values is now legitimate (an absolutely convergent series may be regrouped freely), and gives (2). ∎

The name is a joke about people who use (2) without noticing it needs a proof. Two immediate consequences, both from (2) with $g(x) = ax + b$ and with $g = g_1 + g_2$:

$$\E[aX + b] = a\,\E[X] + b, \qquad \E[g_1(X) + g_2(X)] = \E[g_1(X)] + \E[g_2(X)]. \tag{3}$$

Linearity for sums of different random variables, $\E[X + Y] = \E[X] + \E[Y]$, also holds but needs their joint distribution to state; it comes in module 3. Monotonicity also follows from (2): if $g_1(x) \le g_2(x)$ for all $x \in S_X$ then $\E[g_1(X)] \le \E[g_2(X)]$.

Theorem 2tail-sum formula

If $X$ takes values in $\{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$, then

$$\E[X] = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \P[X > k], \tag{4}$$

with both sides possibly infinite.

Proof

Write $\P[X > k] = \sum_{j > k} P_X(j)$ and exchange the order of summation, which is allowed for non-negative terms (Tonelli for series):

$$\sum_{k=0}^\infty \sum_{j=k+1}^\infty P_X(j) = \sum_{j=1}^\infty P_X(j) \cdot \#\{k : 0 \le k \le j - 1\} = \sum_{j=1}^{\infty} j\, P_X(j) = \E[X].$$

∎

Formula (4) is often easier than (1), because tail probabilities are often easier than point probabilities. For the geometric law, $\P[L > k] = (1-p)^k$, so $\E[L] = \sum_{k \ge 0} (1-p)^k = 1/p$ immediately. On average you wait $1/p$ trials for a success.

Examplea variable with infinite mean

Let $\P[X \ge k] = 1/k$ for $k = 1, 2, \dots$, i.e. $P_X(k) = \frac1k - \frac1{k+1} = \frac{1}{k(k+1)}$. This is a valid PMF (the sum telescopes to $1$), but by (4), $\E[X] = \sum_{k \ge 0} \P[X > k] = \sum_{k \ge 0} \frac{1}{k+1} = \infty$, the harmonic series. Such variables are easy to sample: if $U$ is uniform on $(0,1)$, then $X = \lfloor 1/U \rfloor$ has exactly this law, since $\P[\lfloor 1/U \rfloor \ge k] = \P[1/U \ge k] = \P[U \le 1/k] = 1/k$.

Worked example 2.2.1the mean of the larger of two dice, two ways
Problem

Roll two fair dice and let $X$ be the larger value. Compute $\E[X]$ (a) from the PMF and (b) with the tail-sum formula.

Approach

For a maximum, the CDF is easier than the PMF: $\{X \le k\}$ means both dice are $\le k$. From the CDF either difference to get the PMF, or use $\E[X] = \sum_{k \ge 1}\P[X \ge k]$ (Theorem 2) directly.

Solution

  1. CDF. $\P[X \le k] = \P[\text{both} \le k] = (k/6)^2$ for $k = 0, \dots, 6$.

  2. (a) PMF and mean. $P_X(k) = \frac{k^2 - (k-1)^2}{36} = \frac{2k - 1}{36}$, so $$\E[X] = \sum_{k=1}^6 k\,\frac{2k - 1}{36} = \frac{2\sum k^2 - \sum k}{36} = \frac{2 \cdot 91 - 21}{36} = \frac{161}{36}.$$

  3. (b) Tail sum. $\P[X \ge k] = 1 - \P[X \le k - 1] = 1 - \frac{(k-1)^2}{36}$, so $$\E[X] = \sum_{k=1}^{6}\Big(1 - \frac{(k-1)^2}{36}\Big) = 6 - \frac{0 + 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25}{36} = 6 - \frac{55}{36} = \frac{161}{36}.$$

Result

$\E[X] = 161/36 \approx 4.47$, against $3.5$ for a single die. For maxima, minima and waiting times, the tail-sum route usually saves the differencing step.

§ 03 SpreadVariance and moments

Definitionmoments and variance

The $n$-th moment of $X$ is $\E[X^n]$ and the $n$-th central moment is $\E[(X - \mu_X)^n]$, when they exist. The variance is the second central moment,

$$\Var[X] = \sigma_X^2 = \E\big[(X - \mu_X)^2\big], \tag{5}$$

and the standard deviation is $\sigma_X = \sqrt{\Var[X]}$, which has the units of $X$.

Theorem 3variance identities

If $\E[X^2] < \infty$, then

$$\Var[X] = \E[X^2] - \mu_X^2, \qquad \Var[aX + b] = a^2 \Var[X], \tag{6}$$

and for every constant $c$,

$$\E\big[(X - c)^2\big] = \Var[X] + (\mu_X - c)^2. \tag{7}$$
Proof

First, $\E[X^2] < \infty$ implies $\E[|X|] < \infty$ because $|x| \le 1 + x^2$, so $\mu_X$ exists. Expand the square and use linearity (3): $\E[(X - c)^2] = \E[X^2] - 2c\,\mu_X + c^2$. Adding and subtracting $\mu_X^2$, $\E[(X - c)^2] = (\E[X^2] - \mu_X^2) + (\mu_X - c)^2$. With $c = \mu_X$ the left side is $\Var[X]$, which gives the first identity in (6), and substituting it back gives (7). For the second identity in (6), $aX + b$ has mean $a\mu_X + b$, so $\Var[aX+b] = \E[(aX + b - a\mu_X - b)^2] = a^2\, \E[(X - \mu_X)^2]$. ∎

Identity (7) says the mean is the constant that best predicts $X$ in mean squared error, and the minimal error is the variance. This is the simplest instance of the bias–variance decomposition, and of MMSE estimation (lesson 5.4), where the constant $c$ will become a function of observed data.

For integer-valued laws the factorial moment $\E[X(X-1)]$ is usually easier than $\E[X^2]$, and $\Var[X] = \E[X(X-1)] + \E[X] - \E[X]^2$.

Theorem 4means and variances of the standard laws

$\text{Bernoulli}(p)$: $\mu = p$, $\sigma^2 = p(1-p)$.   $\text{Binomial}(n,p)$: $\mu = np$, $\sigma^2 = np(1-p)$.   $\text{Geometric}(p)$: $\mu = 1/p$, $\sigma^2 = (1-p)/p^2$.   $\text{Poisson}(\alpha)$: $\mu = \sigma^2 = \alpha$.

Proof

Write $q = 1 - p$.

Bernoulli. $\E[X] = 0 \cdot q + 1 \cdot p = p$, and $X^2 = X$, so $\Var[X] = p - p^2$.

Binomial. Use $k\binom nk = n\binom{n-1}{k-1}$ (both count a $k$-subset of $n$ items with a marked member) and $k(k-1)\binom nk = n(n-1)\binom{n-2}{k-2}$ (two marked members, in order). Then

$$\E[K] = \sum_{k=1}^n n\binom{n-1}{k-1}p^k q^{n-k} = np\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{j}p^j q^{n-1-j} = np(p+q)^{n-1} = np,$$

and in the same way $\E[K(K-1)] = n(n-1)p^2 (p+q)^{n-2} = n(n-1)p^2$. So $\Var[K] = n(n-1)p^2 + np - n^2p^2 = np - np^2 = npq$.

Geometric. $\E[L] = 1/p$ by the tail-sum formula above. Differentiating the power series $\sum_{\ell \ge 0} q^\ell = (1-q)^{-1}$ twice term by term (valid inside the radius of convergence $|q| < 1$) gives $\sum_{\ell \ge 2} \ell(\ell-1) q^{\ell - 2} = 2(1-q)^{-3}$. Hence $\E[L(L-1)] = \sum_{\ell} \ell(\ell-1)\, p\, q^{\ell-1} = pq \cdot 2/p^3 = 2q/p^2$, and $\Var[L] = 2q/p^2 + 1/p - 1/p^2 = (2q + p - 1)/p^2 = q/p^2$.

Poisson. $\E[X] = \sum_{x \ge 1} x\frac{\alpha^x e^{-\alpha}}{x!} = \alpha\sum_{x \ge 1}\frac{\alpha^{x-1}e^{-\alpha}}{(x-1)!} = \alpha$, and the same shift by two gives $\E[X(X-1)] = \alpha^2$, so $\Var[X] = \alpha^2 + \alpha - \alpha^2 = \alpha$. ∎

The binomial and Poisson results agree in the Poisson limit: with $p = \alpha/n$, $np(1-p) = \alpha(1 - \alpha/n) \to \alpha$.

§ 04 BoundsMarkov and Chebyshev

Knowing only a mean, or only a mean and a variance, already bounds the tails.

Theorem 5Markov's inequality

If $X \ge 0$ and $a > 0$, then $\displaystyle \P[X \ge a] \le \frac{\E[X]}{a}$.

Proof

Drop the terms with $x < a$ (they are non-negative) and bound $x \ge a$ on the rest:

$$\E[X] = \sum_{x} x\,P_X(x) \ge \sum_{x \ge a} x\,P_X(x) \ge a\sum_{x \ge a} P_X(x) = a\,\P[X \ge a].$$

The same argument with integrals proves it for continuous $X$ (lesson 2.3). ∎

Theorem 6Chebyshev's inequality

If $\Var[X] = \sigma^2 < \infty$ and $c > 0$, then $\displaystyle \P\big[|X - \mu_X| \ge c\big] \le \frac{\sigma^2}{c^2}$.

Proof

$\{|X - \mu_X| \ge c\} = \{(X - \mu_X)^2 \ge c^2\}$. Apply Markov to the non-negative variable $(X - \mu_X)^2$ with $a = c^2$; its expectation is $\sigma^2$. ∎

With $c = k\sigma$: at most $1/k^2$ of the mass is more than $k$ standard deviations from the mean, for any law with finite variance. Exercise 4 shows the bound cannot be improved without more assumptions. For a specific law it is usually loose: for a Gaussian, the probability of $3\sigma$ or more is $0.0027$, against Chebyshev's $0.111$.

Worked example 2.2.2how loose are Markov and Chebyshev?
Problem

Toss a fair coin $100$ times and let $X$ be the number of heads. Bound $\P[X \ge 60]$ with Markov's inequality and $\P[|X - 50| \ge 10]$ with Chebyshev's, and compare with the exact values.

Approach

Both bounds need only moments, which Theorem 4 gives for the binomial. The exact value needs the full PMF, here a finite sum that we evaluate numerically.

Solution

  1. Moments. $X \sim \text{Binomial}(100, \frac12)$, so $\mu = 50$ and $\sigma^2 = 100 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac12 = 25$.

  2. Markov. $X \ge 0$, so $\P[X \ge 60] \le \E[X]/60 = 50/60 \approx 0.833$.

  3. Chebyshev. $\P[|X - 50| \ge 10] \le \sigma^2/10^2 = 25/100 = 0.25$. By symmetry of this binomial about $50$, it also gives $\P[X \ge 60] \le 0.125$.

  4. Exact. $\P[X \ge 60] = \sum_{k=60}^{100}\binom{100}{k}2^{-100} \approx 0.0284$ and $\P[|X - 50| \ge 10] = 2 \times 0.0284 \approx 0.0569$.

Result

Markov is off by a factor $\approx 30$ and Chebyshev by $\approx 4$. Their value is generality (they hold for every law with those moments, and for some laws they are attained), not tightness. Using more information, such as the variance, or later the whole MGF, buys sharper bounds.

§ 06 PracticeExercises

  1. For $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\alpha)$, compute $\E\big[1/(1+X)\big]$.core

    Solution

    By (2), $\sum_{x \ge 0} \frac{1}{x+1}\frac{\alpha^x e^{-\alpha}}{x!} = \frac{e^{-\alpha}}{\alpha}\sum_{x \ge 0}\frac{\alpha^{x+1}}{(x+1)!} = \frac{e^{-\alpha}}{\alpha}(e^\alpha - 1) = \frac{1 - e^{-\alpha}}{\alpha}$. Note that this is not $1/(1 + \E[X])$: in general $\E[g(X)] \ne g(\E[X])$.

  2. Show that $X \sim \text{Uniform}\{1, \dots, n\}$ has mean $\frac{n+1}{2}$ and variance $\frac{n^2-1}{12}$.core

    Solution

    $\E[X] = \frac1n\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ and $\E[X^2] = \frac1n\cdot\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$. By (6), $\Var[X] = \frac{(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} - \frac{(n+1)^2}{4} = \frac{(n+1)\big(2(2n+1) - 3(n+1)\big)}{12} = \frac{(n+1)(n-1)}{12}$.

  3. Show that $\Var[X] = 0$ if and only if $\P[X = \mu_X] = 1$.core

    Solution

    $\Var[X] = \sum_x (x - \mu_X)^2 P_X(x)$ is a sum of non-negative terms, so it vanishes iff $P_X(x) = 0$ for every $x \ne \mu_X$, i.e. iff all mass sits at $\mu_X$. (For general $X$, Chebyshev gives $\P[|X - \mu_X| \ge 1/n] = 0$ for every $n$, and continuity of probability finishes it.)

  4. For $c \ge 1$, let $X$ take the values $-c, 0, c$ with probabilities $\frac{1}{2c^2}, 1 - \frac{1}{c^2}, \frac{1}{2c^2}$. Show that Chebyshev's inequality holds with equality at this $c$.core

    Solution

    By symmetry $\mu_X = 0$, and $\sigma^2 = c^2 \cdot \frac{1}{c^2} = 1$. Then $\P[|X| \ge c] = \frac{1}{c^2} = \sigma^2/c^2$. So no better bound in terms of $\sigma^2$ and $c$ alone is possible.

  5. Show that every median $m$ of $X$ (a point with $\P[X \le m] \ge \tfrac12$ and $\P[X \ge m] \ge \tfrac12$) minimises $c \mapsto \E|X - c|$, assuming $\E|X| < \infty$.stretch

    Solution

    Take $c > m$. Pointwise, $|x - c| - |x - m|$ equals $c - m$ when $x \le m$, and is at least $-(c - m)$ for every $x$ (triangle inequality). So $$\E|X - c| - \E|X - m| \ge (c - m)\,\P[X \le m] - (c - m)\,\P[X > m] = (c - m)\big(2\P[X \le m] - 1\big) \ge 0.$$ The case $c < m$ is symmetric, using $\P[X \ge m] \ge \tfrac12$. Compare (7): squared error picks the mean, absolute error picks the median.
