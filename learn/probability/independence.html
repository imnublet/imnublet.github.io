Independence and sequential experiments

Independence is a statement about numbers, not about causes, and it is what lets probabilities multiply.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 25 min Y&G ch. 1–2
After this lesson you can
  • define independence of two and of $n$ events, and tell it apart from disjointness;
  • construct a counterexample showing pairwise independence does not imply mutual independence;
  • distinguish independence from conditional independence;
  • model repeated trials as a product experiment and derive the binomial law;
  • compute the reliability of series and parallel systems.

§ 01 DefinitionIndependent events

Intuitively, $B$ is irrelevant to $A$ if learning that $B$ occurred does not change the probability of $A$: $\P[A \mid B] = \P[A]$. Multiplying by $\P[B]$ gives a symmetric statement that also makes sense when $\P[B] = 0$, and that is the one we adopt.

Definitionindependence of two events

Events $A$ and $B$ are independent if

$$\P[A \cap B] = \P[A]\,\P[B]. \tag{1}$$

When $\P[B] > 0$, (1) is equivalent to $\P[A \mid B] = \P[A]$, by the definition of conditional probability. Two points are worth stating explicitly.

  • Independence is a property of $\P$, not of the sets. The same two events can be independent under one measure and dependent under another. It need not reflect physical separation either: for one fair die, $A = \{2,4,6\}$ and $B = \{1,2\}$ satisfy $\P[A \cap B] = \tfrac16 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac13$, although both are about the same roll.
  • Disjoint is almost the opposite of independent. If $A \cap B = \varnothing$ with $\P[A], \P[B] > 0$, then $\P[A \cap B] = 0 \ne \P[A]\P[B]$: learning that $B$ occurred tells you for certain that $A$ did not.
Theorem 1complements preserve independence

If $A$ and $B$ are independent, so are $A$ and $B^c$, $A^c$ and $B$, and $A^c$ and $B^c$.

Proof

$A$ is the disjoint union of $A \cap B$ and $A \cap B^c$, so

$$\P[A \cap B^c] = \P[A] - \P[A \cap B] = \P[A] - \P[A]\P[B] = \P[A]\,(1 - \P[B]) = \P[A]\,\P[B^c].$$

By symmetry $A^c$ and $B$ are independent; applying the first result to the independent pair $(A^c, B)$ gives $A^c$ and $B^c$. ∎

Worked example 1.4.1independence is a property of the measure, not the events
Problem

Draw one card from a well-shuffled standard deck. Let $A$ = "ace" and $H$ = "heart". (a) Are $A$ and $H$ independent? (b) Now remove the two of clubs before shuffling. Are they still independent?

Approach

Independence is an equation, $\P[AH] = \P[A]\P[H]$, so check it with numbers; there is no shortcut by intuition about "unrelated" features. Compute each side on the equally likely sample space of cards.

Solution

  1. Full deck. $|\Omega| = 52$; $\P[A] = \frac4{52} = \frac1{13}$, $\P[H] = \frac{13}{52} = \frac14$, and $AH$ is the single card "ace of hearts", so $\P[AH] = \frac1{52} = \frac1{13}\cdot\frac14$. Independent.

  2. Reduced deck. Now $|\Omega| = 51$ with the same aces and hearts: $\P[A] = \frac4{51}$, $\P[H] = \frac{13}{51}$, $\P[AH] = \frac1{51}$.

  3. Compare. $\P[A]\P[H] = \frac{52}{2601} = \frac{52}{51^2}$, whereas $\P[AH] = \frac{51}{51^2}$. These differ, so $A$ and $H$ are not independent. In conditional form, $\P[A \mid H] = \frac{1/51}{13/51} = \frac1{13} \approx 0.0769$, slightly below $\P[A] = \frac4{51} \approx 0.0784$: learning "heart" makes "ace" a little less likely, because removing the two of clubs raises the share of aces among non-hearts to $3/38$ but leaves it at $1/13$ among hearts.

Result

Independent in the full deck, dependent after removing a single unrelated card. The events did not change; the measure did. Independence is a statement about $\P$, and must be checked against the model, not inferred from the physical description.

§ 02 Many eventsPairwise is not enough

For more than two events we must ask for the product rule on every sub-collection.

Definitionmutual independence

Events $A_1, \dots, A_n$ are (mutually) independent if for every index set $J \subseteq \{1, \dots, n\}$ with $|J| \ge 2$,

$$\P\Big[\bigcap_{j \in J} A_j\Big] = \prod_{j \in J} \P[A_j]. \tag{2}$$

That is $2^n - n - 1$ equations. An infinite family is independent if every finite sub-family is.

Examplepairwise but not mutually independent

Toss two fair coins: $\Omega = \{HH, HT, TH, TT\}$, equally likely. Let $A$ = "first is $H$", $B$ = "second is $H$", $C$ = "the two tosses agree" $= \{HH, TT\}$. Each has probability $\tfrac12$, and each pairwise intersection is a single outcome: $A \cap B = A \cap C = B \cap C = \{HH\}$, with probability $\tfrac14 = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12$. So the three pairs are independent. But $A \cap B \cap C = \{HH\}$ too, and $\P[A \cap B \cap C] = \tfrac14 \ne \tfrac18$. Any two of the events determine the third, which three-way independence must forbid.

Conversely, the single equation $\P[A_1 A_2 A_3] = \P[A_1]\P[A_2]\P[A_3]$ does not imply the pairwise ones either (exercise 2), which is why the definition lists every sub-collection. Theorem 1 extends: if $A_1, \dots, A_n$ are independent, replacing any of them by their complements leaves them independent (exercise 3). And independence passes to events built from disjoint groups, e.g. $A_1$ is independent of $A_2 \cup A_3$ (exercise 4).

§ 03 Given a third eventConditional independence

Definitionconditional independence

For $\P[C] > 0$, events $A$ and $B$ are conditionally independent given $C$ if $\P[A \cap B \mid C] = \P[A \mid C]\,\P[B \mid C]$, i.e. if they are independent under the measure $\P[\,\cdot \mid C]$.

Neither notion implies the other. A coin is chosen at random: fair, or two-headed, each with probability $\tfrac12$. It is tossed twice. Given the coin, the tosses are independent. Unconditionally, by the law of total probability,

$$\P[H_1] = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = \tfrac34, \qquad \P[H_1 H_2] = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac14 + \tfrac12 \cdot 1 = \tfrac58 \ne \tfrac{9}{16} = \P[H_1]\P[H_2].$$

The first head is evidence for the two-headed coin, which makes a second head more likely. This is the structure of every latent-variable model: observations are independent given the parameters and dependent after the parameters are integrated out. (For the reverse failure, independence destroyed by conditioning, see the "explaining away" exercise 5.)

§ 04 StagesSequential experiments and Bernoulli trials

Most experiments in this course are built from stages that "do not influence each other". The product construction is what makes that phrase precise.

Theorem 2product experiments

Let $(\Omega_1, p_1)$ and $(\Omega_2, p_2)$ be discrete models (lesson 1.2, Theorem 5). On $\Omega = \Omega_1 \times \Omega_2$, $p(\omega_1, \omega_2) = p_1(\omega_1)\,p_2(\omega_2)$ is a valid model, and for any $A \subseteq \Omega_1$, $B \subseteq \Omega_2$ the events $\tilde A = A \times \Omega_2$ ("stage 1 lands in $A$") and $\tilde B = \Omega_1 \times B$ are independent with $\P[\tilde A] = \P_1[A]$ and $\P[\tilde B] = \P_2[B]$.

Proof

For any $A \subseteq \Omega_1$, $B \subseteq \Omega_2$, since the series have non-negative terms they may be summed iteratively:

$$\P[A \times B] = \sum_{\omega_1 \in A} \sum_{\omega_2 \in B} p_1(\omega_1)\,p_2(\omega_2) = \Big(\sum_{\omega_1 \in A} p_1(\omega_1)\Big)\Big(\sum_{\omega_2 \in B} p_2(\omega_2)\Big) = \P_1[A]\,\P_2[B].$$

With $A = \Omega_1$, $B = \Omega_2$ this gives $\P[\Omega] = 1$, so $p$ is valid. With $B = \Omega_2$ it gives $\P[\tilde A] = \P_1[A]$, and with $A = \Omega_1$, $\P[\tilde B] = \P_2[B]$. Finally $\tilde A \cap \tilde B = A \times B$, so $\P[\tilde A \cap \tilde B] = \P_1[A]\P_2[B] = \P[\tilde A]\P[\tilde B]$. ∎

By induction the same holds for $n$ stages, with mutual independence of events that depend on different stages. The most important case: $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, each a success ($1$) with probability $p$ and a failure ($0$) with probability $1-p$. Then $\Omega = \{0,1\}^n$ and an outcome with $k$ ones has probability $p^k (1-p)^{n-k}$.

Theorem 3binomial law

The probability of exactly $k$ successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli$(p)$ trials is

$$\P[K = k] = \binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}, \qquad k = 0, 1, \dots, n. \tag{3}$$
Proof

The event $\{K = k\}$ is the disjoint union of the singletons $\{\omega\}$, one for each binary string with exactly $k$ ones. Each has probability $p^k(1-p)^{n-k}$ by the product model. Such a string is determined by which $k$ of the $n$ positions hold the ones, and there are $\binom nk$ ways to choose $k$ positions out of $n$ (proved in lesson 1.5). Finite additivity gives (3). As a check, $\sum_k \binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k} = (p + 1 - p)^n = 1$ by the binomial theorem. ∎

Worked example 1.4.2Bernoulli trials for a test suite
Problem

A model is evaluated on $10$ test prompts. Each prompt is passed with probability $0.9$, independently of the others. Find (a) the probability that at least $9$ are passed and (b) the probability that the first failure happens on prompt $4$.

Approach

Independent trials with a common success probability are a Bernoulli sequence (Theorem 2). "Number of successes" is binomial (Theorem 3); "first failure at a given trial" is a specific sequence prefix, whose probability is a product.

Solution

  1. Model. Let $K$ be the number of passes. By Theorem 3, $\P[K = k] = \binom{10}{k}(0.9)^k(0.1)^{10 - k}$, $k = 0, \dots, 10$.

  2. At least nine. The events $\{K = 9\}$ and $\{K = 10\}$ are disjoint, so $$\P[K \ge 9] = \binom{10}{9}(0.9)^9(0.1) + (0.9)^{10} = 0.3874 + 0.3487 = 0.7361.$$

  3. First failure on prompt 4. This is the event "pass, pass, pass, fail" on the first four prompts, with prompts $5$–$10$ unrestricted. By independence (Theorem 2) its probability is $(0.9)^3(0.1) = 0.0729$.

Result

(a) $\P[K \ge 9] \approx 0.736$: even with a $90\%$ per-prompt pass rate, about one run in four fails two or more prompts. (b) $0.0729$. The general pattern, $\P[\text{first failure at } \ell] = (0.9)^{\ell-1}(0.1)$, is the geometric law of lesson 2.1.

§ 05 ApplicationReliability of series and parallel systems

A system is built from $n$ components that fail independently, component $i$ working with probability $p_i$. Let $W_i$ be the event "component $i$ works".

  • Series: the system works only if all components work. By (2), $\P[\text{works}] = \P\big[\bigcap_i W_i\big] = \prod_{i} p_i$.
  • Parallel: the system works if at least one works. By De Morgan and independence of the complements (Theorem 1, extended), $\P[\text{works}] = 1 - \P\big[\bigcap_i W_i^c\big] = 1 - \prod_{i} (1 - p_i)$.

Series systems are weaker than their weakest link; parallel systems are stronger than their strongest. Ten components in series, each with $p = 0.99$, work with probability $0.99^{10} \approx 0.904$. Duplicate each component in parallel and every stage works with probability $1 - 0.01^2 = 0.9999$, so the system works with probability $0.9999^{10} \approx 0.9990$: redundancy cut the failure probability from about $9.6\%$ to $0.1\%$. Any series–parallel network is evaluated by applying the two rules from the inside out, provided the blocks share no components.

§ 07 PracticeExercises

  1. Show that $A$ is independent of itself if and only if $\P[A] \in \{0, 1\}$. Deduce that an event of probability $0$ or $1$ is independent of every event.core

    Solution

    $\P[A \cap A] = \P[A]$, so independence means $\P[A] = \P[A]^2$, i.e. $\P[A] \in \{0,1\}$. If $\P[A] = 0$ then $0 \le \P[A \cap B] \le \P[A] = 0 = \P[A]\P[B]$. If $\P[A] = 1$ then $\P[A^c] = 0$ is independent of $B$, hence so is $A$ by Theorem 1.

  2. Roll two fair dice. Let $A$ = "first die is 1, 2 or 3", $B$ = "first die is 3, 4 or 5", $C$ = "the sum is 9". Show that $\P[ABC] = \P[A]\P[B]\P[C]$ but that no two of the events are independent.core

    Solution

    $\P[A] = \P[B] = \tfrac12$ and $C = \{(3,6),(4,5),(5,4),(6,3)\}$, so $\P[C] = \tfrac19$. $ABC = \{(3,6)\}$, so $\P[ABC] = \tfrac1{36} = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac19$. But $\P[AB] = \P[\text{first} = 3] = \tfrac16 \ne \tfrac14$, $\P[AC] = \P\{(3,6)\} = \tfrac1{36} \ne \tfrac1{18}$ and $\P[BC] = \P\{(3,6),(4,5),(5,4)\} = \tfrac1{12} \ne \tfrac1{18}$.

  3. Prove that if $A_1, \dots, A_n$ are independent, then so are $A_1^c, A_2, \dots, A_n$. Conclude that any choice of complements preserves independence.core

    Solution

    We must check (2) for every $J$ with $|J| \ge 2$. If $1 \notin J$ nothing changed. If $1 \in J$, let $J' = J \setminus \{1\} \ne \varnothing$ and $D = \bigcap_{j \in J'} A_j$. Since $D$ is the disjoint union of $A_1 \cap D$ and $A_1^c \cap D$, $$\P[A_1^c \cap D] = \P[D] - \P[A_1 \cap D] = \prod_{j \in J'} \P[A_j] - \P[A_1]\prod_{j \in J'} \P[A_j] = \P[A_1^c] \prod_{j \in J'} \P[A_j],$$ using (2) for $J'$ (or the trivial identity if $|J'| = 1$) and for $J$. Repeating the argument one index at a time handles any set of complements.

  4. Let $A, B, C$ be independent. Show that $A$ is independent of $B \cup C$.core

    Solution

    By distributivity and inclusion–exclusion, $\P[A \cap (B \cup C)] = \P[AB] + \P[AC] - \P[ABC] = \P[A]\big(\P[B] + \P[C] - \P[B]\P[C]\big)$, and the bracket is $\P[B] + \P[C] - \P[BC] = \P[B \cup C]$.

  5. Explaining away. Two independent fair coins are tossed; $A$ = "first is $H$", $B$ = "second is $H$", $C$ = "at least one $H$". Show that $A$ and $B$ are independent but not conditionally independent given $C$.core

    Solution

    $\P[C] = \tfrac34$, $\P[A \mid C] = \P[B \mid C] = \tfrac{1/2}{3/4} = \tfrac23$ and $\P[AB \mid C] = \tfrac{1/4}{3/4} = \tfrac13 \ne \tfrac49$. Given that at least one head occurred, learning the first was a tail forces the second to be a head.

  6. Show that the binomial PMF (3) increases in $k$ while $k < (n+1)p$ and decreases afterwards, so its mode is $\lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor$ (two modes when $(n+1)p$ is an integer).stretch

    Solution

    For $1 \le k \le n$, with $b_k = \P[K = k]$, $$\frac{b_k}{b_{k-1}} = \frac{\binom nk}{\binom n{k-1}} \cdot \frac{p}{1-p} = \frac{(n-k+1)\,p}{k\,(1-p)}.$$ This is $> 1$ iff $(n-k+1)p > k(1-p)$ iff $(n+1)p > k$, and $= 1$ iff $k = (n+1)p$. So $b_k$ rises while $k < (n+1)p$, then falls; the largest term is at $k = \lfloor (n+1)p \rfloor$, tied with $k - 1$ when $(n+1)p$ is an integer.
