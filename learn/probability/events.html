Experiments, outcomes and events

Before any number is assigned, a model has to say what can happen. This lesson builds that vocabulary from sets.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 20 min Y&G ch. 1
After this lesson you can
  • model an experiment by a sample space $\Omega$ at the right level of detail;
  • manipulate events with set operations and prove identities such as De Morgan's laws;
  • decompose any event along a partition of $\Omega$;
  • say which collections of events a probability can live on (a $\sigma$-algebra), and why one is needed.

§ 01 ModelExperiments and sample spaces

Probability theory never talks about the world directly. It talks about a model of a procedure whose result we cannot predict: toss a coin three times, measure a resistor, wait for the next packet. We call such a procedure an experiment. What makes it an experiment in the technical sense is not the physics but the list of things we agree to record.

Definitionoutcome, sample space

An outcome $\omega$ is one complete, indivisible result of the experiment. The sample space $\Omega$ is the set of all outcomes. Its elements must be

  • mutually exclusive: one run of the experiment produces exactly one $\omega$, and
  • collectively exhaustive: every run produces some $\omega \in \Omega$.

The same physical procedure admits many sample spaces, and choosing one is a modelling decision. Toss a coin three times. If we record the sequence,

$$\Omega_1 = \{H,T\}^3 = \{HHH, HHT, HTH, HTT, THH, THT, TTH, TTT\}, \qquad |\Omega_1| = 8.$$

If we record only the number of heads, $\Omega_2 = \{0,1,2,3\}$. The map $f:\Omega_1 \to \Omega_2$ that counts heads shows that $\Omega_2$ is a coarsening of $\Omega_1$: every question about $\Omega_2$ can be asked of $\Omega_1$, but not the other way round ("was the first toss heads?" has no answer in $\Omega_2$). The rule of thumb is to pick the finest space you will need and no finer, because later the equally-likely assumption is often natural on the fine space ($\Omega_1$) and false on the coarse one ($\Omega_2$).

Sample spaces need not be finite. Tossing until the first head gives $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, countably infinite. Measuring a waiting time gives $\Omega = [0, \infty)$, uncountable. All three cases matter in this course, and the uncountable one is what forces the extra care of § 04.

§ 02 EventsEvents are sets

We rarely care about individual outcomes. We ask whether something happened: "at least two heads", "the voltage exceeded 5 V". Each such statement is true for some outcomes and false for others, so it is identified with the set of outcomes where it is true.

Definitionevent

An event is a subset $A \subseteq \Omega$. The event $A$ occurs on a run of the experiment if the outcome $\omega$ of that run satisfies $\omega \in A$. The empty set $\varnothing$ is the impossible event; $\Omega$ is the certain event.

Logic on statements becomes algebra on sets. For events $A, B \subseteq \Omega$:

  • "not $A$" is the complement $A^c = \{\omega \in \Omega : \omega \notin A\}$;
  • "$A$ or $B$" is the union $A \cup B = \{\omega : \omega \in A \text{ or } \omega \in B\}$;
  • "$A$ and $B$" is the intersection $A \cap B$, often written $AB$;
  • "$A$ but not $B$" is the difference $A \setminus B = A \cap B^c$;
  • "$A$ implies $B$" is inclusion $A \subseteq B$;
  • $A$ and $B$ are disjoint (mutually exclusive) if $A \cap B = \varnothing$: they cannot occur on the same run.

Unions and intersections extend to any indexed family $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$: $\bigcup_i A_i$ is the set of outcomes in at least one $A_i$, and $\bigcap_i A_i$ the set of outcomes in every $A_i$.

Theorem 1De Morgan's laws

For any family of events $\{A_i\}_{i \in I}$,

$$\Big(\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i\Big)^c = \bigcap_{i \in I} A_i^c, \qquad \Big(\bigcap_{i \in I} A_i\Big)^c = \bigcup_{i \in I} A_i^c. \tag{1}$$
Proof

Two sets are equal when each contains the other, so we chase an arbitrary element. For the first identity,

$$\omega \in \Big(\bigcup_i A_i\Big)^c \iff \neg\,(\exists i : \omega \in A_i) \iff \forall i : \omega \notin A_i \iff \forall i : \omega \in A_i^c \iff \omega \in \bigcap_i A_i^c.$$

The middle step is the logical rule $\neg \exists \equiv \forall \neg$. For the second identity, apply the first to the family $\{A_i^c\}$ and use $(A^c)^c = A$: $\big(\bigcup_i A_i^c\big)^c = \bigcap_i A_i$; taking complements of both sides gives $\bigcup_i A_i^c = \big(\bigcap_i A_i\big)^c$. ∎

The same element-chasing proves the distributive laws $A \cap \big(\bigcup_i B_i\big) = \bigcup_i (A \cap B_i)$ and $A \cup \big(\bigcap_i B_i\big) = \bigcap_i (A \cup B_i)$, which we use constantly.

§ 03 StructurePartitions

The most useful way to break a hard question into easy ones is to split $\Omega$ into cases.

Definitionpartition

A countable family of events $\{B_1, B_2, \dots\}$ is a partition of $\Omega$ (Yates & Goodman call it an event space) if the $B_i$ are pairwise disjoint, $B_i \cap B_j = \varnothing$ for $i \ne j$, and $\bigcup_i B_i = \Omega$.

A partition is exactly a coarser sample space: every outcome lands in exactly one $B_i$, so "which $B_i$ occurred" is itself a mutually exclusive, collectively exhaustive description of the result.

Theorem 2decomposition along a partition

If $\{B_i\}$ is a partition of $\Omega$ and $A \subseteq \Omega$ is any event, then the events $C_i = A \cap B_i$ are pairwise disjoint and

$$A = \bigcup_i C_i = \bigcup_i (A \cap B_i). \tag{2}$$
Proof

Disjointness. For $i \ne j$, $C_i \cap C_j = A \cap (B_i \cap B_j) = A \cap \varnothing = \varnothing$.

Union. By the distributive law, $\bigcup_i (A \cap B_i) = A \cap \big(\bigcup_i B_i\big) = A \cap \Omega = A$. ∎

Once probabilities are additive over disjoint sets (next lesson), (2) becomes $\P[A] = \sum_i \P[A \cap B_i]$, and with conditioning it becomes the law of total probability.

§ 04 TechnicalitiesWhich subsets are events?

On a finite or countable $\Omega$ every subset can be an event. On $\Omega = [0,1]$ that is too much to ask: there is no function on all subsets of $[0,1]$ that is countably additive, invariant under translation (mod 1) and gives an interval its length. (The Vitali construction, which uses the axiom of choice, produces such a non-measurable set.) The fix is to fix in advance a collection of subsets that will be called events, closed under the operations we need.

Definitionσ-algebra

A collection $\mathcal F$ of subsets of $\Omega$ is a $\sigma$-algebra if

  1. $\Omega \in \mathcal F$;
  2. $A \in \mathcal F \Rightarrow A^c \in \mathcal F$;
  3. $A_1, A_2, \dots \in \mathcal F \Rightarrow \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty A_i \in \mathcal F$.

Closure under countable intersections follows: if $A_i \in \mathcal F$ then $A_i^c \in \mathcal F$, so $\bigcup_i A_i^c \in \mathcal F$, so its complement, which by (1) is $\bigcap_i A_i$, is in $\mathcal F$. Also $\varnothing = \Omega^c \in \mathcal F$, and finite unions are countable unions padded with $\varnothing$. In short: any event you can describe with countably many "and", "or" and "not" operations is still an event.

In practice

For countable $\Omega$ take $\mathcal F = 2^\Omega$, all subsets. For $\Omega = \R^n$ take the Borel sets, the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing all open boxes. Every set you will meet in this course is Borel, so after this section we stop mentioning $\mathcal F$ unless it matters.

§ 05 In codeEvents as Python sets

For finite experiments, building $\Omega$ explicitly and treating events as frozensets is the fastest way to check an identity or a count.

§ 06 PracticeExercises

  1. Show that $A \cup B$ is the disjoint union of $A$ and $B \setminus A$, and that $B$ is the disjoint union of $A \cap B$ and $B \setminus A$.core

    Solution

    $A \cap (B \setminus A) = A \cap B \cap A^c = \varnothing$. And $A \cup (B \cap A^c) = (A \cup B) \cap (A \cup A^c) = (A \cup B) \cap \Omega = A \cup B$ by distributivity. For the second, $(A \cap B) \cap (B \cap A^c) = B \cap (A \cap A^c) = \varnothing$ and $(B \cap A) \cup (B \cap A^c) = B \cap (A \cup A^c) = B$. These two decompositions are the whole proof of inclusion–exclusion in the next lesson.

  2. Prove that $\{A,\; B \setminus A,\; (A \cup B)^c\}$ is a partition of $\Omega$ for any events $A, B$.core

    Solution

    By Exercise 1, $A$ and $B \setminus A$ are disjoint with union $A \cup B$. The third set is the complement of $A \cup B$, so it is disjoint from both (each is a subset of $A \cup B$), and the union of all three is $(A \cup B) \cup (A \cup B)^c = \Omega$.

  3. Find the smallest $\sigma$-algebra on $\Omega$ that contains a given event $A$.core

    Solution

    Any $\sigma$-algebra containing $A$ must contain $\Omega$, $A^c$ and $\varnothing = \Omega^c$, so it contains $\{\varnothing, A, A^c, \Omega\}$. That collection is closed under complements (they pair up) and under unions (any union of its members is again one of the four), so it is a $\sigma$-algebra, and therefore the smallest.

  4. Let $\{B_1, \dots, B_n\}$ be a finite partition with every $B_i \ne \varnothing$. Show that the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing every $B_i$ has exactly $2^n$ elements.stretch

    Solution

    Let $\mathcal G = \{\bigcup_{i \in S} B_i : S \subseteq \{1,\dots,n\}\}$. Any $\sigma$-algebra containing the $B_i$ is closed under finite unions, so it contains $\mathcal G$. Conversely $\mathcal G$ is a $\sigma$-algebra: $\Omega$ corresponds to $S = \{1,\dots,n\}$; the complement of $\bigcup_{i \in S} B_i$ is $\bigcup_{i \notin S} B_i$ because the $B_i$ partition $\Omega$; and a union of members indexed by $S_1, S_2, \dots$ is the member indexed by $\bigcup_k S_k$. Finally the map $S \mapsto \bigcup_{i\in S} B_i$ is injective because the $B_i$ are disjoint and non-empty (pick $\omega_i \in B_i$; then $\omega_i$ is in the union iff $i \in S$). Hence $|\mathcal G| = |2^{\{1,\dots,n\}}| = 2^n$.
