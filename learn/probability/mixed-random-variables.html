Mixed random variables and the delta function

Pass a Gaussian through a ReLU and you get a variable that is neither discrete nor continuous. Deltas handle both at once.

Probability & multivariate data analysis 25 min Y&G ch. 4
After this lesson you can
  • recognise random variables that are neither discrete nor continuous, and read their atoms off the CDF;
  • prove that a CDF has at most countably many jumps and split it into a jump part and a continuous part;
  • define the Dirac delta as a limit of pulses and prove its sifting property;
  • write one generalised density that covers discrete, continuous and mixed variables, and compute expectations with it;
  • derive the mean and variance of a rectified Gaussian, $\max(0, Y)$.

§ 01 MotivationA ReLU makes an atom

Let $Y \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ be the pre-activation of a unit and $X = \max(0, Y)$ its ReLU output. Every negative $Y$ is mapped to the single value $0$, so

$$\P[X = 0] = \P[Y \le 0] = \Phi(-\mu/\sigma) > 0.$$

$X$ is not continuous (a continuous variable gives every point probability zero). Nor is it discrete: for $0 < a < b$, $\P[a < X \le b] = \P[a < Y \le b]$ is spread smoothly over the interval. Its CDF is

$$F_X(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & x < 0, \\ \Phi\big((x - \mu)/\sigma\big), & x \ge 0, \end{cases} \tag{1}$$

with a jump of height $\Phi(-\mu/\sigma)$ at $0$ and a smooth rise after. Saturating sensors, clipped gradients, censored survival times, rainfall (zero on dry days) and insurance claims all have the same shape. The CDF handles them already; this lesson builds the matching notion of a density.

§ 02 StructureJumps and the rest

Theorem 1a CDF has countably many jumps

The set $D = \{x : \P[X = x] > 0\}$ of atoms of any random variable $X$ is countable.

Proof

Let $D_n = \{x : \P[X = x] > 1/n\}$. If $D_n$ contained $n$ distinct points $x_1, \dots, x_n$, the disjoint events $\{X = x_i\}$ would have total probability greater than $n \cdot \frac1n = 1$, which is impossible. So $|D_n| < n$. Then $D = \bigcup_{n \ge 1} D_n$ is a countable union of finite sets, hence countable. ∎

List the atoms as $x_1, x_2, \dots$ with masses $p_i = \P[X = x_i]$, and define the jump part $F_d(x) = \sum_{i : x_i \le x} p_i$ and the continuous part $F_c = F_X - F_d$.

Theorem 2jump–continuous decomposition

$F_c$ is continuous and non-decreasing, so every CDF splits as $F_X = F_d + F_c$, a pure-jump part plus a continuous part.

Proof

Non-decreasing. Fix $a < b$ and any finite set of atoms $x_{i_1} < \dots < x_{i_m}$ in $(a, b]$. The events $\{X = x_{i_k}\}$ are disjoint subsets of $\{a < X \le b\}$, so $\sum_k p_{i_k} \le F_X(b) - F_X(a)$. Taking the supremum over finite sets, $F_d(b) - F_d(a) = \sum_{a < x_i \le b} p_i \le F_X(b) - F_X(a)$, which says $F_c(a) \le F_c(b)$.

Continuous. $F_d$ is right-continuous: $F_d(x + h) - F_d(x) = \sum_{x < x_i \le x + h} p_i \to 0$ as $h \downarrow 0$, because each term eventually drops out and the series is dominated by $\sum p_i \le 1$. Its left limit is $F_d(x^-) = \sum_{x_i < x} p_i$ by the same argument, so $F_d$ jumps by exactly $\P[X = x]$ at $x$ (and by $0$ at non-atoms). $F_X$ is right-continuous and jumps by $\P[X = x]$ at $x$ (lesson 2.3, Theorem 1). The difference $F_c$ is therefore right-continuous with jumps $\P[X = x] - \P[X = x] = 0$, i.e. continuous. ∎

Fine print

A continuous $F_c$ need not have a density: the Cantor function is continuous, rises from $0$ to $1$, and has derivative $0$ almost everywhere. Lebesgue's decomposition splits $F_c$ further into an absolutely continuous part (with a density) and such a singular part. Singular laws do not occur in engineering models, and in this course $F_c$ will always be $\int_{-\infty}^x f_c$ for some $f_c \ge 0$.

Worked example 2.5.1an insurance claim with an atom at zero
Problem

In a year, a policy has no claim with probability $0.7$. Otherwise the claim amount is exponential with mean $2000$. Let $X$ be the claim amount (with $X = 0$ if no claim). Find $F_X$, its jump–continuous decomposition, $\P[X > 1000]$ and the median of $X$.

Approach

Condition on whether there is a claim and use the law of total probability for each $\{X \le x\}$. The atom shows up as a jump in $F_X$; the rest is continuous.

Solution

  1. CDF by cases. For $x < 0$, $F_X(x) = 0$. For $x \ge 0$, with $C$ = "a claim is made", $$F_X(x) = \P[X \le x \mid C^c]\,\P[C^c] + \P[X \le x \mid C]\,\P[C] = 0.7 + 0.3\big(1 - e^{-x/2000}\big).$$

  2. Decomposition. $F_X$ jumps by $0.7$ at $0$ and is continuous elsewhere, so $F_d(x) = 0.7\,u(x)$ and $F_c(x) = 0.3\big(1 - e^{-x/2000}\big)\,u(x)$, with $F_X = F_d + F_c$. The atom is $\P[X = 0] = F_X(0) - F_X(0^-) = 0.7$.

  3. Tail. $\P[X > 1000] = 1 - F_X(1000) = 0.3\,e^{-0.5} \approx 0.182$.

  4. Median. A median $m$ needs $\P[X \le m] \ge \frac12$ and $\P[X \ge m] \ge \frac12$. $F_X(0) = 0.7 \ge \frac12$ and $\P[X \ge 0] = 1$, so $m = 0$; and any $m > 0$ fails the second condition because $\P[X \ge m] \le 0.3$.

Result

$F_X(x) = \big(1 - 0.3e^{-x/2000}\big)u(x)$, an atom of $0.7$ at zero plus a continuous part of total mass $0.3$; $\P[X > 1000] \approx 0.18$; median $0$. A model that ignores the atom (e.g. fits one exponential to all policies) gets the median, and every small-claim probability, badly wrong.

§ 03 ToolThe Dirac delta

We would like a "density" for an atom: something that is zero away from $x_0$, yet integrates to one. No function does that, but a limit of ordinary functions does what we need inside integrals.

Definitionunit pulse, delta, unit step

For $\varepsilon > 0$ let $\delta_\varepsilon(x) = \frac1\varepsilon$ for $|x| < \varepsilon/2$ and $0$ otherwise, a pulse of unit area. The Dirac delta $\delta(x)$ is the symbol defined by its action on functions $g$ that are continuous at $x_0$:

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x)\,\delta(x - x_0) \d x := \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0}\int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x)\,\delta_\varepsilon(x - x_0) \d x. \tag{2}$$

The unit step is $u(x) = 1$ for $x \ge 0$ and $0$ for $x < 0$.

Theorem 3sifting property

If $g$ is continuous at $x_0$ (and bounded near it), the limit in (2) exists and

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x)\,\delta(x - x_0) \d x = g(x_0). \tag{3}$$
Proof

The integral against the pulse is the average of $g$ over an interval: $\int g(x)\delta_\varepsilon(x - x_0)\d x = \frac1\varepsilon\int_{x_0 - \varepsilon/2}^{x_0 + \varepsilon/2} g(x)\d x$. Given $\eta > 0$, continuity at $x_0$ gives $\rho > 0$ with $|g(x) - g(x_0)| < \eta$ for $|x - x_0| < \rho$. For $\varepsilon < 2\rho$,

$$\Big|\frac1\varepsilon\int_{x_0 - \varepsilon/2}^{x_0 + \varepsilon/2} g(x)\d x - g(x_0)\Big| \le \frac1\varepsilon\int_{x_0 - \varepsilon/2}^{x_0 + \varepsilon/2} |g(x) - g(x_0)|\d x \le \eta.$$

As $\eta$ was arbitrary, the limit is $g(x_0)$. ∎

With $g = \ind\{x \le t\}$, which is continuous at $0$ whenever $t \ne 0$, (3) gives $\int_{-\infty}^t \delta(x)\d x = u(t)$ for $t \ne 0$. So $\delta$ is the derivative of the unit step in this generalised sense: a jump of height $p$ in a CDF differentiates to $p\,\delta$. (At the jump itself the integral is ambiguous; we resolve it below by the CDF's right-continuity.) Any other family of unit-area pulses that shrinks to $x_0$, Gaussians $\mathcal N(x_0, \varepsilon^2)$ for instance, gives the same limit (3), which is why the delta is the natural model of an impulse in signal processing.

§ 04 UnificationGeneralised densities

Definitiongeneralised PDF

If $F_X = F_d + F_c$ with atoms $p_i$ at $x_i$ and $F_c(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x f_c(t)\d t$, the generalised PDF of $X$ is

$$f_X(x) = f_c(x) + \sum_i p_i\, \delta(x - x_i), \tag{4}$$

and for any $g$ continuous at every atom with $\int |g| f_c + \sum_i |g(x_i)|p_i < \infty$,

$$\E[g(X)] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x) f_X(x) \d x = \int_{-\infty}^\infty g(x) f_c(x) \d x + \sum_i p_i\, g(x_i). \tag{5}$$

The second equality is (3) applied term by term. Formula (5) is the Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral $\int g \d F_X$ written in engineering notation, and it reduces to the two cases we already have: a discrete $X$ has $f_c = 0$ and (5) is lesson 2.2's sum; a continuous one has no atoms and (5) is lesson 2.3's integral. A PMF is a comb of deltas. Probabilities follow from (5) with $g = \ind_B$, with the convention that an atom on the boundary of $B$ counts if it lies in $B$: $\P[X \le x] = \int_{-\infty}^{x^+} f_X$, consistent with right-continuity.

Worked example 2.5.2expected payout with and without a deductible
Problem

For the claim $X$ of worked example 2.5.1, write the generalised PDF and compute $\E[X]$, $\Var[X]$ and the expected payout $\E[\max(X - 500, 0)]$ under a $500$ deductible.

Approach

Write $f_X$ as in (4), then use (5): each expectation is an integral against the continuous part plus a sum over atoms. Here the atom sits at $0$, so its contribution vanishes whenever $g(0) = 0$.

Solution

  1. Generalised PDF. With $\lambda = 1/2000$, $$f_X(x) = 0.7\,\delta(x) + 0.3\,\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\,\ind\{x > 0\}.$$

  2. Mean and second moment. By (5) with $g(x) = x$ and $g(x) = x^2$, the atom contributes $0.7 \cdot g(0) = 0$, and the exponential moments $1/\lambda$, $2/\lambda^2$ give $$\E[X] = 0.3 \cdot 2000 = 600, \qquad \E[X^2] = 0.3 \cdot 2 \cdot 2000^2 = 2.4 \times 10^6.$$

  3. Variance. $\Var[X] = 2.4 \times 10^6 - 600^2 = 2.04 \times 10^6$, so $\sigma_X \approx 1428$.

  4. Deductible. $g(x) = \max(x - 500, 0)$ is continuous with $g(0) = 0$, so only the continuous part contributes. Substituting $y = x - 500$, $$\E[g(X)] = 0.3\int_{500}^\infty (x - 500)\,\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\d x = 0.3\,e^{-500\lambda}\int_0^\infty y\,\lambda e^{-\lambda y}\d y = 600\,e^{-0.25} \approx 467.$$

Result

$\E[X] = 600$, $\sigma_X \approx 1428$, and the deductible cuts the expected payout from $600$ to $\approx 467$. The standard deviation is more than twice the mean: the atom at zero and the exponential tail together make the claim very dispersed.

§ 05 ApplicationThe rectified Gaussian

Theorem 4moments of a ReLU of a Gaussian

Let $Y \sim \mathcal N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, $X = \max(0, Y)$ and $m = \mu/\sigma$. Then

$$f_X(x) = \Phi(-m)\,\delta(x) + \frac1\sigma\,\phi\Big(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\Big)\, \ind\{x > 0\}, \tag{6}$$ $$\E[X] = \mu\,\Phi(m) + \sigma\,\phi(m), \qquad \E[X^2] = (\mu^2 + \sigma^2)\,\Phi(m) + \mu\sigma\,\phi(m). \tag{7}$$
Proof

Density. From (1), $F_X$ has one atom, at $0$, of mass $\Phi(-m)$. For $x > 0$, $F_X(x) = \Phi((x - \mu)/\sigma)$ has derivative $\frac1\sigma\phi((x - \mu)/\sigma)$, and for $x < 0$ it is constant. That gives (6).

Moments. By (5), the atom contributes $0^k\,\Phi(-m) = 0$ to $\E[X^k]$ for $k \ge 1$, so $\E[X^k] = \int_0^\infty x^k \frac1\sigma\phi\big(\frac{x-\mu}\sigma\big)\d x$. Substitute $x = \mu + \sigma z$; the lower limit becomes $z = -m$:

$$\E[X^k] = \int_{-m}^\infty (\mu + \sigma z)^k\, \phi(z) \d z.$$

Three integrals appear. $\int_{-m}^\infty \phi = 1 - \Phi(-m) = \Phi(m)$. Since $\phi' = -z\phi$, $\int_{-m}^\infty z\phi(z)\d z = \big[-\phi(z)\big]_{-m}^\infty = \phi(-m) = \phi(m)$. And integrating by parts as in lesson 2.4, $\int_{-m}^\infty z^2\phi(z)\d z = \big[-z\phi(z)\big]_{-m}^\infty + \int_{-m}^\infty \phi = -m\,\phi(m) + \Phi(m)$. Hence

$$\E[X] = \mu\Phi(m) + \sigma\phi(m), \qquad \E[X^2] = \mu^2\Phi(m) + 2\mu\sigma\phi(m) + \sigma^2\big(\Phi(m) - m\phi(m)\big),$$

and $\sigma^2 m = \mu\sigma$ turns the second into (7). ∎

Sanity checks: as $\mu \to \infty$ with $\sigma$ fixed, $\Phi(m) \to 1$ and $\phi(m) \to 0$, so $\E[X] \to \mu$ (the ReLU is almost always in its linear regime); as $\mu \to -\infty$, $\E[X] \to 0$. For the standard case $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$: $\E[X] = \phi(0) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi} \approx 0.399$ and $\E[X^2] = \tfrac12$. Formulas (7) are exactly what moment-propagation methods for Bayesian neural networks push through each ReLU layer, and they explain why He initialisation scales weights by $\sqrt{2/\text{fan-in}}$: a ReLU of a centred input keeps only half of the second moment.

§ 06 In codeExpectations with an atom

§ 07 PracticeExercises

  1. Show that $X = \max(0, Z)$ with $Z \sim \mathcal N(0,1)$ has $\Var[X] = \frac12 - \frac1{2\pi} \approx 0.341$.core

    Solution

    By (7) with $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$: $\E[X] = \phi(0) = 1/\sqrt{2\pi}$ and $\E[X^2] = \Phi(0) = \tfrac12$. So $\Var[X] = \tfrac12 - \tfrac1{2\pi}$.

  2. A request times out after $c$ seconds: the recorded latency is $X = \min(T, c)$ with $T \sim \text{Exponential}(\lambda)$. Write the generalised PDF of $X$ and compute $\E[X]$.core

    Solution

    $\P[X = c] = \P[T \ge c] = e^{-\lambda c}$, and on $[0, c)$ $X$ has the density of $T$: $f_X(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}\ind\{0 \le x < c\} + e^{-\lambda c}\delta(x - c)$. By the tail formula (lesson 2.3, exercise 5), $\E[X] = \int_0^c \P[T > x]\d x = (1 - e^{-\lambda c})/\lambda$. The same value from (5): $\int_0^c x\lambda e^{-\lambda x}\d x + c\,e^{-\lambda c}$.

  3. $X$ has CDF $F_X(x) = 0$ for $x < 0$, $\frac{1 + x}{4}$ for $0 \le x < 1$, and $1$ for $x \ge 1$. Find the atoms, the generalised PDF and $\E[X]$.core

    Solution

    Jump at $0$: $F(0) - F(0^-) = \tfrac14$. Jump at $1$: $F(1) - F(1^-) = 1 - \tfrac24 = \tfrac12$. Between, slope $\tfrac14$. So $f_X(x) = \tfrac14\delta(x) + \tfrac14\ind\{0 < x < 1\} + \tfrac12\delta(x - 1)$, which integrates to $\tfrac14 + \tfrac14 + \tfrac12 = 1$, and $\E[X] = 0 + \int_0^1 \tfrac x4\d x + \tfrac12 = \tfrac18 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac58$.

  4. Show that $\delta(ax) = \delta(x)/|a|$ for $a \ne 0$, in the sense that $\lim_{\varepsilon \to 0}\int g(x)\,\delta_\varepsilon(ax)\d x = g(0)/|a|$ for $g$ continuous at $0$.core

    Solution

    Substitute $y = ax$, $\d x = \d y/|a|$ (the absolute value appears because for $a < 0$ the limits swap): $\int g(x)\delta_\varepsilon(ax)\d x = \frac1{|a|}\int g(y/a)\,\delta_\varepsilon(y)\d y \to \frac{g(0)}{|a|}$ by Theorem 3 applied to $y \mapsto g(y/a)$, which is continuous at $0$.

  5. A sensor saturates: it reports $X = \max(-c, \min(c, Y))$ for $Y \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2)$. Find the atoms and show that $\E[X^2] = \sigma^2\big(1 - 2Q(k) - 2k\,\phi(k)\big) + 2c^2 Q(k)$, where $k = c/\sigma$.stretch

    Solution

    Atoms at $\pm c$, each of mass $\P[Y > c] = Q(k)$. In between, $X = Y$. By (5) and symmetry, $\E[X^2] = 2c^2Q(k) + \sigma^2\int_{-k}^{k} z^2\phi(z)\d z$. Integrating by parts as in Theorem 4, $\int_{-k}^{k} z^2\phi = \big[-z\phi(z)\big]_{-k}^{k} + \int_{-k}^{k}\phi = -2k\phi(k) + 1 - 2Q(k)$. As $c \to \infty$ this tends to $\sigma^2$, and the deficit measures the power lost to clipping.
